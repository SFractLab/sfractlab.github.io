Open questions

Level: AdvancedBuilds on Minibrots & self-similarity and Angles, addresses & trees

Douady and Hubbard proved in 1982 that the Mandelbrot set is connected. More than forty years later, the basic questions about its fine structure are still open: whether it is locally connected, whether hyperbolic parameters are dense, and whether its boundary has positive area. This chapter states them precisely, as of October 2026, says what is known and why they are hard, and lists the recent progress.

Local connectivity

The Mandelbrot set is connected, as Douady and Hubbard proved in 1982 (why it is connected). A set is locally connected if it is connected also on the smallest scales. Zoom in on a point x of the set, as far as you like. The set is locally connected at x if the parts of it near the center of the view are always joined to x inside the view, not only by way of parts outside it, and it is locally connected if this holds at each of its points; equivalently, every point has arbitrarily small connected neighborhoods within the set. A comb whose teeth crowd toward one tooth is connected, but not locally connected at the points of that tooth above the base: however far one zooms in on such a point, other teeth pass close to it, and they are joined to it only through the base, far outside the view. Pictures cannot settle the question for M, because at every magnification some of its parts are thinner than a pixel.

Douady and Hubbard’s note of January 1982 already called it likely that the map Ψ below extends continuously to the circle, which by Carathéodory’s theorem is the same question (Douady and Hubbard, 1982, §4). In November 1982 Douady asked it outright, « L’ensemble M est-il localement connexe ? », as the fourth of the open questions of his Bourbaki seminar (Douady, Séminaire Bourbaki, p. 48; the history chapter).

MLC matters because it has several equivalent forms, each of which would complete a part of the theory (Schleicher, fibers and local connectivity; Zakeri, §2):

  • the map Ψ from the outside of the unit disk onto the outside of M extends continuously to the unit circle (Carathéodory’s theorem), so that every parameter ray lands and its landing point depends continuously on the angle (irrational angles);
  • every fiber of M, in Schleicher’s sense, is a single point: roughly, any two points of M can be separated by cutting along pairs of rational parameter rays that land together;
  • M is homeomorphic to the pinched disk built from Lavaurs’ chords (a model of the whole set), so its topology would be completely described by external angles.

Douady and Hubbard showed that MLC implies the density of hyperbolicity, the next question below. Lasse Rempe and Dierk Schleicher noticed that a formally weaker statement already suffices: that every boundary point of M is the landing point of at least one parameter ray (Rempe and Schleicher, 2008).

What Yoccoz proved

Around 1990 Jean-Christophe Yoccoz introduced puzzles for quadratic polynomials, building on Bodil Branner and John Hubbard’s work on cubic ones. For c in M outside the main cardioid, the external rays that land at the fixed point α, together with an equipotential, cut a disk around the filled Julia set into finitely many pieces. Pulling these pieces back under the map gives smaller and smaller pieces, nested around every point of the Julia set. Where the nested pieces shrink to a point, the Julia set is locally connected; a similar construction in the parameter plane, the parapuzzle, does the same for M. Yoccoz never published the proofs himself; John Hubbard wrote them up (Hubbard, three theorems of J.-C. Yoccoz, 1993), and Milnor gave expository lectures (Milnor, local connectivity of Julia sets). The theorems, for c in M:

  • If z2 + c is not infinitely renormalizable, then M is locally connected at c.
  • If, in addition, it has no neutral cycle, its filled Julia set is locally connected.
  • M is also locally connected at every parameter with a neutral cycle, even when the Julia set is not.

Yoccoz also proved an inequality that forces the limbs to be small, Yoccoz’s inequality. For c in the p/q-limb, q rays land at α and the map rotates them; then a logarithm τ of the multiplier of α lies in the closed disk of radius log 2 / q in the right half-plane that touches the imaginary axis at 2πip/q (Hubbard, Theorem I.B). Since c = λ/2 − λ2/4 with λ = eτ, the whole limb lies in the image of that disk.

The hard case: infinite renormalization

By Yoccoz’s theorem, MLC can fail only at parameters that are infinitely renormalizable: parameters that lie in infinitely many nested small copies of M (renormalization and straightening). At such a parameter some iterate of the map, restricted to a small disk, is again quadratic-like, and so on at every scale. The strategy for these parameters, developed by Dennis Sullivan, Curtis McMullen and Mikhail Lyubich, is to prove a priori bounds. Each renormalization is a quadratic-like map from a small disk-shaped region U onto a larger one V. The ring between them (an annulus) is like a protective collar around the small filled Julia set inside U. How thick the collar is is measured by a number, its modulus, which is a different use of the word than the modulus |⁠z| of a complex number. For a round ring r < |⁠z| < R it is log(R/r)/2π, and a holomorphic change of coordinates does not change it (it is conformally invariant), so it measures the shape of the ring, not its size. A priori bounds say that at every level, however deep, the collar can be chosen with modulus at least one fixed number, so the small Julia sets are never squeezed against their surroundings. Such bounds give compactness: rescaled to a common size, the renormalizations of all levels belong to one family in which every sequence has a convergent subsequence (a compact family), so they cannot degenerate. Combined with rigidity theorems, which show that no other parameter has the same combinatorics, the same pattern of nested copies and of rays that land together, this gives MLC at that parameter: its fiber is a single point. The danger is degeneration: near satellite components and parabolic parameters the annuli can become arbitrarily thin.

The proofs have advanced class by class:

  • Lyubich (1997): parameters of sufficiently high type whose renormalizations satisfy a “secondary limbs condition” (Lyubich, Acta Mathematica).
  • Jeremy Kahn (2006) and Kahn and Lyubich (2008–2009): bounded primitive combinatorics, then decorations and molecules (Kahn; Kahn and Lyubich).
  • Dzmitry Dudko and Lyubich (2023): some infinitely renormalizable parameters of bounded satellite type, the first examples of this kind (Geometric and Functional Analysis 33).
  • Dudko and Lyubich (2026): every infinitely renormalizable parameter of bounded type, including the classical Feigenbaum point at the end of the period-doubling cascade (Publications mathématiques de l’IHÉS 143).
  • Preprints of 2026, not yet refereed: Kahn and Lyubich for “elephant eye” combinatorics, whose small copies may approach the cusp (arXiv), and Kahn, Alex Kapiamba and Lyubich for a primitive class that may approach the cusp (arXiv).

Dzmitry Dudko’s note for the International Congress of Mathematicians 2026 maps the whole area (Dudko); Anna Miriam Benini’s survey is a gentler introduction (Benini).

Density of hyperbolicity

A parameter is hyperbolic when the critical orbit is attracted to an attracting cycle or escapes. The conjecture says that hyperbolic parameters are dense in the plane; equivalently, every component of the interior of M is a hyperbolic component. A non-hyperbolic interior component, a “queer” component, would have to be strange indeed: by a theorem of Ricardo Mañé, Paulo Sad and Dennis Sullivan (1983), its parameters would have Julia sets of positive area carrying an invariant line field, a choice of a line through almost every point of a positive-area part of the Julia set that the map preserves. So the conjecture is equivalent to: no quadratic Julia set carries an invariant line field (McMullen, Complex Dynamics and Renormalization, Theorem 4.9 and Corollary 4.10).

The question is old. In 1920 Fatou conjectured that maps whose critical points are attracted to cycles are typical, an early form of the conjecture (the history chapter), and Douady and Hubbard’s 1982 note even asserted that the interior of M consists of the parameters with an attracting cycle, citing a forthcoming paper by Paulo Sad; the claim was soon demoted to a conjecture (the history chapter). For real parameters the conjecture was proved in 1997 by Mikhail Lyubich and, independently, by Jacek Graczyk and Grzegorz Świątek (Graczyk and Świątek), and for real polynomials of every degree by Oleg Kozlovski, Weixiao Shen and Sebastian van Strien (Annals of Mathematics, 2007). Every component of the interior of M that meets the real axis is hyperbolic (McMullen, Corollary 1.9).

The area of the boundary

The boundary of M has Hausdorff dimension 2 (Shishikura), but whether it has positive area is open; it is conjectured to be zero. Part of the answer is known: the parameters on the boundary that are not renormalizable at all form a set of zero area (Shishikura; a new proof by Avila and Moreira), so a boundary of positive area would have to come from renormalizable parameters. Floating-point computations that cover the plane with squares, not yet formally verified, leave room for an area of at most about 0.025 (area, dimension and famous numbers).

What a computer can decide

Can a program draw M to any prescribed precision? Peter Hertling showed that it can if density of hyperbolicity holds (Hertling, 2005); without that assumption the question is open. For Julia sets the answer is settled in outline: filled Julia sets are always computable (Braverman and Yampolsky, 2008), while some Julia sets are not, and these examples have Siegel disks (Braverman and Yampolsky, 2006). The computing chapter explains what pictures can and cannot prove.

Questions anyone can state

  • Does the vertical line through −3/4 meet M anywhere except at −3/4 itself? (π in the Mandelbrot set)
  • The centers of period n are the roots of a polynomial with integer coefficients, after the factors of smaller periods are divided out. Is that polynomial irreducible over the rationals? This is a long-standing conjecture (Buff, Floyd, Koch and Parry, Conjecture 1.4), confirmed by computation for small n (Hutz and Towsley, §3; counting components).
  • Is the Burning Ship set connected? Are Mandelbulbs connected? (pictures without theorems)
  • Is every Multibrot set locally connected? This is MLC in higher degree (Multibrot sets).

Key ideas

  • As of October 2026, MLC, the density of hyperbolicity and the area of the boundary of M are open; positive-area Julia sets and the computability of Julia sets are settled.
  • MLC is equivalent to every parameter ray landing continuously, to all fibers being points, and to M being the pinched-disk model; it implies density of hyperbolicity.
  • Yoccoz proved MLC at every parameter that is not infinitely renormalizable; his inequality confines each p/q-limb to a region that shrinks as q grows.
  • The remaining cases are infinitely renormalizable; they are attacked through a priori bounds, class by class, most recently at all bounded-type parameters including the Feigenbaum point.
  • Density of hyperbolicity is equivalent to the absence of invariant line fields on quadratic Julia sets; it is proved on the real line.

Check yourself

  1. Why can MLC fail only at infinitely renormalizable parameters?

    Yoccoz’s theorem proves local connectivity of M at every parameter that is not infinitely renormalizable, so any point where it fails must lie in infinitely many nested small copies of M.

  2. Check that the Yoccoz region of the 1/2-limb reaches exactly to −2.

    For q = 2 the disk has radius (log 2)/2 and touches the imaginary axis at πi, so its farthest point from the axis is τ = log 2 + πi. Then λ = eτ = −2 and c = λ/2 − λ2/4 = −1 − 1 = −2, the tip, which lies in the 1/2-limb.

  3. MLC implies that every parameter ray lands. Why is this stronger than what is already known about rays?

    Rays at rational angles are known to land, and so are the rays at almost every angle, by Fatou’s theorem on radial limits. MLC would make every ray land, at every angle, with the landing point depending continuously on the angle.

  4. What would a counterexample to density of hyperbolicity look like?

    An open set of parameters inside M, a “queer” component, whose maps have no attracting cycle. By the theorem of Mañé, Sad and Sullivan their Julia sets would have positive area and carry an invariant line field.
