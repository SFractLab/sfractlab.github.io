Chaos, nature, and fractals

Level: Introductory

A rule with nothing random in it can still defeat prediction, and a simple construction can produce detail at every scale. These two discoveries, deterministic chaos and fractal geometry, changed how scientists think about weather, planets, and living populations. The Mandelbrot and Julia sets are where the two meet most clearly.

Determined, yet unpredictable

In 1814 Pierre-Simon Laplace imagined an intelligence that knew, at one instant, every force in nature and the position of every body. If it could analyze all those data, “nothing would be uncertain and the future, as the past, would be present to its eyes” (Laplace’s Philosophical Essay on Probabilities, in the 1902 translation). The argument needs perfect knowledge of the present. Chaos is the discovery of how much that word “perfect” matters.

Henri Poincaré met the difficulty in the motion of three bodies under gravity. His memoir on the problem won King Oscar II’s prize in January 1889. While it was being printed he found a serious error, had the printed copies destroyed at a cost greater than the prize money, and published a corrected memoir in 1890 (the Institut Mittag-Leffler’s account of the prize). The correction revealed orbits so tangled that, in 1899, he did not even attempt to draw them. In 1908 he summarized the lesson: “it may happen that small differences in the initial conditions produce very great ones in the final phenomena. A small error in the former will produce an enormous error in the latter. Prediction becomes impossible” (Poincaré, Science and Method, translated 1914).

A system is deterministic when its present state and its rule fix the entire future; nothing is left to chance. It is chaotic when, in addition, tiny differences between starting states grow rapidly, typically exponentially, until they are as large as the states themselves. Chaos is therefore not the absence of a rule. It is a property of certain rules, and it makes long-range prediction impossible in practice even though the future is fixed in principle.

Errors that double: an exact example

The clearest example comes from the family studied in this atlas. Take c = −2, the leftmost point of the Mandelbrot set, and follow real starting points x between −2 and 2 under x ↦ x2 − 2. Writing x as 2 cos 2πθ, where the angle θ is measured in full turns, turns the rule into something transparent:

(2 cos 2πθ)2 − 2 = 2 cos 2π(2θ).

One step of the map doubles the angle. In binary, doubling moves every digit of θ one place to the left and drops the digit that passes the binary point. After n steps, the position depends on the digits of the starting angle from the nth onward. Knowing the start to ten decimal places, about 33 binary digits, therefore fixes the orbit for only about 33 steps. After that the orbit is governed by digits nobody measured.

The average rate at which nearby orbits separate is the Lyapunov exponent λ: an initial error δ grows roughly like δeλn. Here λ = ln 2, one binary digit lost per step. The time until the error reaches a tolerance Δ is about (1/λ) ln(Δ/δ), so it grows only with the logarithm of the precision. Measuring the start a thousand times more precisely adds only ten steps. That is why chaos limits prediction in practice: precision is expensive, and its reward is small. ChaosBook’s chapter on Lyapunov exponents gives the general definitions.

Two remarks connect this example to later chapters. The same angle doubling organizes the external rays of every quadratic map. And the critical orbit at c = −2 is not chaotic at all: 0 ↦ −2 ↦ 2 ↦ 2 lands on a fixed point after two steps, which makes −2 a Misiurewicz parameter. Almost every other starting point in [−2, 2] wanders as in the figure.

Lorenz and the butterfly

Around 1961 the meteorologist Edward Lorenz was running a twelve-variable weather model on a small computer at MIT. To repeat part of a run, he typed in numbers from a printout. In his own words, “the computer was carrying its numbers to about six decimal places, but, in order to have twelve numbers together on one line, I had instructed it to round off the printed values to three places.” The repeated run soon departed from the original, the difference “doubling in magnitude in about four simulated days” (Lorenz’s Kyoto Prize lecture).

His 1963 paper, Deterministic Nonperiodic Flow, distilled the phenomenon into three equations from a model of convection, with σ = 10, b = 8/3 and r = 28:

dx/dt = σ(y − x), dy/dt = x(r − z) − y, dz/dt = xy − bz.

Its typical solutions never settle down and never repeat, and the paper concluded that prediction “of the sufficiently distant future is impossible by any method, unless the present conditions are known exactly.”

The butterfly arrived later. In 1963 Lorenz recalled a meteorologist’s objection to an older theory, that cyclones arise from the instability of a uniform flow: if the theory were correct, “one flap of a sea gull’s wings would be enough to alter the course of the weather forever.” The controversy, Lorenz added, “has not yet been settled, but the most recent evidence seems to favor the sea gulls” (The Predictability of Hydrodynamic Flow). For a talk in December 1972 the session’s convener, Philip Merilees, chose the title “Does the flap of a butterfly’s wings in Brazil set off a tornado in Texas?” Lorenz did not answer yes. A flap that can help start a tornado, he said, “can equally well be instrumental in preventing a tornado”, and small disturbances change the sequence of weather events rather than how often they occur (the 1972 talk).

Lorenz’s solutions also show a second feature of chaos. They are drawn towards a bounded set, an attractor, and stay on it; only their position along it is unpredictable. On average, an error in that position is multiplied by e in about 1.1 time units, since the largest Lyapunov exponent is about 0.906. The motion stretches nearby states apart and folds the stretched region back onto itself, the mechanism that Stephen Smale captured in 1960 in his horseshoe map (Smale, 1967). Repeated stretching and folding layers the attractor like pastry dough, so that a cross-section shows a Cantor-like structure of layers. David Ruelle and Floris Takens called such attractors “strange” in 1971. The Lorenz attractor’s estimated dimension is about 2.06: a little more than a surface. Whether the equations really have this attractor, rather than a very long transient, was settled only in 1999, by Warwick Tucker’s computer-assisted proof, published in full in 2002.

What “chaos” means

The word entered mathematics with a 1975 paper by Tien-Yien Li and James Yorke, Period Three Implies Chaos. For a continuous map of an interval, a cycle of period three forces cycles of every period and an uncountable set of orbits that wander irregularly. Oleksandr Sharkovsky had proved a more general ordering of periods in 1964, in a paper that remained little known outside the Soviet Union for years.

There is still no single definition. A widely used one is Robert Devaney’s (1986): a map is chaotic on a set if

  • it has sensitive dependence: arbitrarily close to every point there are points whose orbits eventually separate from it by at least some fixed distance;
  • it is topologically transitive: every region of the set eventually reaches every other region;
  • its periodic points are dense: every region contains a periodic orbit.

In 1992 Banks and coauthors showed that, on an infinite set, the second and third conditions already imply the first (Banks et al., On Devaney’s Definition of Chaos). Physicists usually call a bounded motion chaotic when its largest Lyapunov exponent is positive. Hunt and Ott’s Defining Chaos compares the definitions.

Chaos is not randomness. A chaotic orbit is completely determined by its start, but its statistics can be, in Robert May’s words, “indistinguishable from the sample function of a random process.” Nor does a computer reproduce a chaotic orbit: rounding errors grow like any other error, so a long computed orbit soon differs from the true orbit with the same start. What the computation can still deliver is a typical orbit. For some systems a true orbit with a slightly different start stays close to the computed one for a long time, a property called shadowing; for typical chaotic maps it holds over long but finite stretches (Hammel, Yorke and Grebogi, 1988). Pictures of attractors are meaningful for this reason; a computed long-range forecast for one particular start is not.

The road to chaos: period doubling

In 1976 the ecologist Robert May surveyed the map x ↦ rx(1 − x), a simple model of a population with limited resources (May, Simple mathematical models with very complicated dynamics). As r increases, a stable equilibrium gives way to a stable 2-cycle, then a 4-cycle, an 8-cycle and so on, and finally to chaos. He closed with a plea: “we would all be better off if more people realised that simple nonlinear systems do not necessarily possess simple dynamical properties.”

This logistic map is the real quadratic family of this atlas in other coordinates. Substituting z = r(1/2 − x) turns it into z ↦ z2 + c with c = r(2 − r)/4, so its famous bifurcation diagram describes the real line of the Mandelbrot set:

In 1978 Mitchell Feigenbaum found that the lengths of successive period-doubling intervals shrink by a ratio approaching δ = 4.6692016…, and that the same number appears for a wide class of maps with a single quadratic hump. Pierre Coullet and Charles Tresser found this universality independently. It predicts something measurable in systems whose equations nobody knows. In 1979–80 Albert Libchaber and Jean Maurer saw period doubling up to period 16 in convecting liquid helium, and later measurements gave δ ≈ 4.4 in mercury convection and 4.4 ± 0.1 in an electronic circuit: close to the theory, given that only a few doublings can be resolved (Physics Today, 1981; Libchaber, Laroche and Fauve; Linsay). The minibrots chapter explains the scaling.

Order and chaos are interleaved on this line in a remarkable way. Michael Jakobson proved in 1981 that the parameters with chaotic behavior, in a precise statistical sense, form a set of positive length (Jakobson). Yet Mikhail Lyubich and, independently, Jacek Graczyk and Grzegorz Świątek proved in 1997 that parameters with an attracting cycle are dense: every interval of c, however short, contains a periodic window (Lyubich’s density theorem). Lyubich later showed that almost every real parameter is one of the two kinds, periodic or chaotic. No picture can display this: the windows are mostly far narrower than a pixel. The periodicity chapter follows the cascade in the complex plane.

Chaos in nature

A real system is never known exactly, and its equations are only approximations. Evidence for chaos in nature therefore comes in three strengths. The strongest is a well-tested set of equations, computed carefully, as for the planets. Next come laboratory experiments under controlled conditions that follow a predicted route, such as period doubling. The weakest is statistical analysis of measurements from the field.

Weather and climate

Lorenz argued in 1969 that errors in the smallest motions of the atmosphere spread to ever larger ones, so that two nearly equal states become as different as two randomly chosen states “within a finite time interval, which cannot be lengthened by reducing the amplitude of the initial error” (Lorenz, 1969). A 2019 study estimated the intrinsic limit for midlatitude weather at about two weeks. Forecasts are useful to about ten days today, against seven about thirty years earlier, and reducing the initial error tenfold would gain up to five days (Zhang et al., 2019). Forecast centers have responded with ensembles. Since December 1992, the European Centre for Medium-Range Weather Forecasts and the US National Meteorological Center have run many forecasts from slightly different starts, and the spread of the results shows how far the forecast can be trusted (25 years of ensemble forecasting at ECMWF).

Climate is a different question. Lorenz distinguished predictions of the first kind, of the sequence of states, from predictions of the second kind, of how the statistics respond to changed conditions such as doubled carbon dioxide (Lorenz, Climatic Predictability, 1975). Chaos limits the first kind; it does not by itself rule out the second, just as the Lorenz attractor keeps its shape while positions on it become unpredictable.

The solar system

In 1988 Gerald Sussman and Jack Wisdom followed the outer planets for 845 million years on a special-purpose computer and found Pluto’s orbit to be chaotic, with errors growing by a factor of e about every 20 million years (Sussman and Wisdom, 1988). In 1989 Jacques Laskar found the inner solar system chaotic, with a Lyapunov time of about five million years (Laskar, 1989). His illustration: “An error of 15 meters in the initial position of the Earth gives rise to an error of about 150 meters after 10 Ma, but the same error becomes 150 million km after 100 Ma” (Laskar, Is the Solar System Stable?).

Chaos is not the same as instability. Of 2,501 computed futures of the solar system over five billion years, about 1% let Mercury’s orbit become eccentric enough for a collision with Venus or the Sun; the rest stay orderly (Laskar and Gastineau, 2009). Smaller bodies are chaotic too, some of them on human timescales. Saturn’s moon Hyperion has a regular orbit, but its spin tumbles chaotically, as predicted in 1984 and confirmed with Voyager 2 images (Wisdom, Peale and Mignard; Black, Nicholson and Thomas). In the asteroid belt, a chaotic zone near the 3:1 resonance with Jupiter matches the Kirkwood gap: asteroids there wander onto orbits that cross that of Mars (Wisdom, 1983).

The laboratory

Controlled experiments give the cleanest evidence, because they can be repeated and their parameters varied. Besides the convection experiments, period doubling and chaos were observed in driven electronic circuits, and in 1983 Leon Chua designed a simple circuit in order to produce chaos (Matsumoto, A chaotic attractor from Chua’s circuit, 1984). Hermann Haken showed in 1975 that the equations of a single-mode laser have the form of Lorenz’s equations, and Lorenz-like chaos was later observed in a laser (Weiss and Brock, 1986). In a stirred chemical reactor running the Belousov–Zhabotinsky reaction, Roux, Simoyi and Swinney reconstructed a strange attractor from measured concentrations in 1983 (their paper). The double pendulum is the classroom example: in one measurement its Lyapunov exponent was 7.5 ± 1.5 per second, so an error grows by a factor of e in about a seventh of a second (Shinbrot et al., 1992).

Living systems

May’s population models raised the question whether real populations are chaotic. In one experiment with flour beetles, a model predicted which rates of adult recruitment, set by the experimenters, would make the population settle, oscillate or behave chaotically, and the cultures followed the predicted sequence (Costantino et al., 1997). A plankton community from the Baltic Sea, kept in the laboratory under constant conditions for 2,319 days, fluctuated irregularly with a positive Lyapunov exponent; its species could be predicted only 15 to 30 days ahead (Benincà et al., 2008). A 2022 analysis of 172 population time series from the field found signs of chaos in more than 30% of them, most often in plankton and insects, least often in birds and mammals. Many of the series are short, however, which makes such counts uncertain (Rogers, Johnson and Munch, 2022).

Other claims remain open or were withdrawn. Measles epidemics were argued to be chaotic in the 1980s. Later work explained their major changes with a seasonally driven epidemic model and changing birth rates, and whether the irregularity is chaos remains unresolved (Earn et al., 2000). Heart cells stimulated periodically in the laboratory show period doubling and irregular rhythms, as theory predicts (Guevara, Glass and Shrier, 1981). Whether a healthy heartbeat is chaotic, a popular claim, is disputed: in 2009 the journal Chaos put the question to several research groups and published their answers side by side (Glass, Is the normal heart rate chaotic?, 2009).

A Lyapunov time is the time for an error to grow by a factor of e. The time until a forecast becomes useless is several Lyapunov times, depending on the initial error and the tolerance, as in the doubling example.

How a claim of chaos is tested

Measured series are short, noisy, and recorded while conditions change. The usual methods reconstruct an attractor from one measured quantity and estimate its dimension or Lyapunov exponents, or test whether forecasts made from similar past states lose accuracy as fast as chaos predicts (Sugihara and May, 1990). Each method can be fooled. Jean-Pierre Eckmann and David Ruelle showed that a dimension estimated from N data points cannot exceed about 2 log10 N, so a thousand points can never reveal a dimension above six (Eckmann and Ruelle, 1992). A low-dimensional “climatic attractor” reported in 1984 did not survive a closer analysis, and several claims of chaos in brain waves and heartbeats failed tests against surrogate data: artificial series with the same statistics but no deterministic rule.

Chaotic models, noisy systems. “The weather is chaotic” is a statement about the best models of the atmosphere, supported by the way real forecast errors grow. Real systems are usually best described as chaotic dynamics plus noise, and deciding how much of an irregular record is chaos and how much is noise is often the hardest part.

Fractals: detail at every scale

Mathematicians built shapes with detail at every scale long before they had a name: Karl Weierstrass’s continuous function without a tangent anywhere (1872), Georg Cantor’s set (1883), Helge von Koch’s curve (1904) and Wacław Sierpiński’s triangle (1915). They were not welcome. Charles Hermite wrote in 1893 that he turned away “with fright and horror from this lamentable plague of continuous functions that have no derivatives”, and Poincaré complained in 1908 that logic sometimes breeds monsters (Science et méthode, in French).

Their size is measured by a dimension. Halving a segment leaves 2 copies, halving a square 4 = 22, halving a cube 8 = 23: the exponent is the dimension. The Koch curve consists of 4 copies of itself at one third of the size, so 3D = 4 and D = log 4 / log 3 ≈ 1.26, more than a curve and less than an area. Each stage multiplies its length by 4/3, so the finished curve is infinitely long, although the snowflake made of three such curves encloses only 8/5 of the area of its first triangle. Felix Hausdorff defined in 1918 a dimension for every set, the Hausdorff dimension, which agrees with the similarity dimension for self-similar sets like these (Hutchinson, 1981).

Benoit Mandelbrot turned these curiosities into a geometry of nature. In 1967 he asked How Long Is the Coast of Britain? Lewis Fry Richardson had found that a coastline’s measured length keeps growing as the ruler shrinks. Mandelbrot read Richardson’s data as a dimension: 1.25 for the west coast of Britain, 1.13 for Australia’s coast, and 1.02 for South Africa’s, one of the smoothest coasts in the atlas. The coastline is not made of exact copies of itself, but each piece resembles the whole in a statistical sense. In 1975 he coined the word fractal, from the Latin fractus, broken or irregular, and his book The Fractal Geometry of Nature (1982) made the idea widely known.

There is no accepted definition of a fractal. Mandelbrot first proposed one in terms of dimension and later moved to looser descriptions. Kenneth Falconer suggests treating the word like “life” in biology: a fractal usually has detail at arbitrarily small scales, is too irregular for classical geometry, has some form of self-similarity, perhaps approximate or statistical, has a fractal dimension larger than its topological dimension, and often has a simple, perhaps recursive, definition. A non-integer dimension is not required: the boundary of the Mandelbrot set has dimension exactly two.

Fractals in nature, within limits

Coastlines, river networks, the branching airways of the lungs, and the outlines of clouds have all been described by fractal scaling. Rain and cloud areas observed by radar and satellite follow one area–perimeter law from about 1 to 1000 kilometers across (Lovejoy, 1982). Particles that drift at random and stick on contact grow into branching clusters of dimension about 1.7, a model for growth processes such as electrodeposition (Witten and Sander, 1981). The spiral cones of Romanesco cauliflower repeat on several scales, and in 2021 biologists traced this to growth points that repeatedly start to form flowers and then fail (Azpeitia et al., 2021).

Natural fractals hold only between a smallest and a largest scale. A survey of 96 reports of fractals in physics experiments found that the scaling typically extended over only about 1.3 powers of ten (Avnir et al., Is the geometry of nature fractal?, 1998). Mandelbrot replied that “fractals are not a panacea; they are not everywhere,” while pointing to fractured metals that scale over five powers of ten. A mathematical fractal, by contrast, keeps its structure at every magnification.

Where chaos and fractals meet

The two ideas meet in at least two places. Strange attractors are fractal, as the layered Lorenz attractor shows. And when a system has several possible long-term fates, the boundary between the starting points that lead to each of them is often fractal, so that near the boundary the fate depends on ever finer details of the start (McDonald, Grebogi, Ott and Yorke, 1985).

For z ↦ z2 + c in the complex plane, both happen at once. The Julia set is the boundary between starting points that escape to infinity and those that stay bounded, and on it the map is chaotic in Devaney’s sense: repelling periodic points are dense in it, and any small piece of it is eventually spread over all of it (Milnor’s lectures, Theorem 11.1 and Corollary 11.2). Off the Julia set, in the Fatou set, the iteration is orderly. The example above, c = −2, is an extreme case: its Julia set is the whole interval [−2, 2], chaotic but not at all fractal-looking.

Pierre Fatou and Gaston Julia developed this theory between 1917 and 1920, and Julia’s memoir won the 1918 Grand Prix of the Paris Academy of Sciences (Julia, 1918). They could only draw rough sketches; accurate pictures had to wait for computers. Robert Brooks and Peter Matelski drew a first, crude computer picture of the set in a paper written for a 1978 conference and published in 1981; Mandelbrot published his own pictures in 1980; and in 1982 Adrien Douady and John Hubbard proved that the set is connected and named it after Mandelbrot. The history chapter tells this story in more detail. The set is not exactly self-similar: its small copies are distorted, and its resemblance to Julia sets near Misiurewicz points is a limiting statement. The next chapter begins with the arithmetic behind it all.

Key ideas

  • A deterministic system can be unpredictable in practice: in chaotic motion small errors grow exponentially, at a rate measured by the Lyapunov exponent.
  • For x ↦ x2 − 2 the error, measured as an angle, doubles at every step, so each extra step of prediction costs one more binary digit of the start.
  • There is no single definition of chaos; Devaney’s asks for sensitive dependence, topological transitivity and dense periodic points.
  • Many systems reach chaos through period doubling, whose interval lengths shrink by a ratio approaching Feigenbaum’s δ ≈ 4.669, and the logistic map is the real line of the Mandelbrot set in other coordinates.
  • A fractal has detail at every scale; its size is measured by a dimension, such as the similarity dimension log N / log s. Natural objects are fractal only over a limited range of scales.
  • Chaos and fractals meet in Julia sets: the map is chaotic on a set that is fractal.

Check yourself

  1. For x ↦ x2 − 2, you measure the starting point a thousand times more precisely. How many more steps can you predict?

    About ten. The error doubles at each step, and 210 = 1024 ≈ 1000, so a thousand times smaller error reaches the same size about ten steps later.

  2. Compute the similarity dimensions of the Cantor set, the Koch curve and the Sierpiński triangle.

    The Cantor set consists of 2 copies scaled by 1/3: log 2 / log 3 ≈ 0.631. The Koch curve consists of 4 copies scaled by 1/3: log 4 / log 3 ≈ 1.262. The Sierpiński triangle consists of 3 copies scaled by 1/2: log 3 / log 2 ≈ 1.585.

  3. The logistic map first doubles its period at r = 3. Which parameter c of z2 + c does that correspond to, and where is it in the Mandelbrot set?

    c = r(2 − r)/4 = 3 · (−1)/4 = −3/4. It is the point where the period-two bulb is attached to the main cardioid.

  4. In z2 + c, the period doubles at c = −3/4 and again at −5/4. If every later interval were shorter than the previous one by the factor δ ≈ 4.669, where would the cascade end? Compare with the true value.

    The intervals would add up to 0.5 · (1 + 1/δ + 1/δ2 + …) = 0.5 · δ/(δ − 1) ≈ 0.636, so the cascade would end near −0.75 − 0.636 ≈ −1.386. The true end is −1.4012: the early ratios are smaller than δ (the first is about 4.23), and only approach it in the limit.

  5. Rotating the circle by an irrational fraction of a full turn sends every orbit densely around the circle. Is this map chaotic in Devaney’s sense?

    No. It is topologically transitive, but a rotation keeps the distance between any two points constant, so it has no sensitive dependence, and since no point ever returns exactly, it has no periodic points at all.
