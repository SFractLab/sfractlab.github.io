External rays and angles

Level: IntermediateBuilds on Julia sets and Cardioids, bulbs & periods

Outside every connected filled Julia set, and outside the Mandelbrot set, there is a natural system of coordinates: a potential that says how fast points escape, and an angle. The curves of constant angle, the external rays, give landmarks such as tips, branch points and the roots of bulbs addresses made of fractions, and doubling those fractions mirrors the dynamics.

The escape potential

An escape-time picture counts the steps an orbit needs to leave a large disk. A finer measure is how fast it leaves. Far from the origin, z2 + c is almost z2, so log⁠|⁠z| roughly doubles at each step. Dividing by 2n removes the doubling and leaves the escape potential of the filled Julia set Kc, also called its Green’s function:

Gc(z) = limn→∞ 2−n log⁺|⁠fcn(z)|,
Gc(fc(z)) = 2Gc(z),

where log⁺r = max(log r, 0). The potential is zero exactly on Kc, positive outside it, and continuous (Milnor’s lectures, §17). In electrostatics it would be the potential around a charged conductor shaped like Kc, and its level curves are called equipotentials. A computer needs only a few steps: once |⁠zn| is large, 2−n log|⁠zn| is already a close estimate, and the boundaries between the colored bands of an escape-time picture approximate equipotentials.

Böttcher’s coordinate: squaring in disguise

Near infinity, z2 + c is not just close to squaring: it is squaring, seen in another coordinate. This is a theorem of Lucjan Böttcher, usually dated to his Russian paper of 1904, although the result already appears in a Polish paper of his from 1898; Böttcher only sketched the proof, which Ritt and Fatou completed around 1920 (Domoradzki and Stawiska tell its history). There is a unique coordinate w = φc(z), defined for large |⁠z| and with φc(z)/z → 1 as z → ∞, in which the map becomes w ↦ w2 (Milnor’s lectures, Theorem 6.7 and §17):

φc(fc(z)) = φc(z)2.

The two polar coordinates of w become two coordinates for z. The modulus gives the potential, Gc(z) = log|⁠φc(z)|. The argument, measured in whole turns, is the external angle of z.

For c = 0 the coordinate is the identity, φ0(z) = z: equipotentials are circles and the curves of constant angle are straight lines. For c = −2 it is almost as explicit. Substituting z = w + 1/w turns squaring into z2 − 2, because (w + 1/w)2 − 2 = w2 + 1/w2. So φ−2 is the inverse of w ↦ w + 1/w, which flattens the unit circle onto the segment [−2, 2], the Julia set.

The coordinate reaches all the way down to the Julia set exactly when the orbit of 0 stays bounded. Then φc maps the whole outside of Kc one-to-one onto the outside of the closed unit disk, preserving angles (Milnor’s lectures, Theorem 17.3). If 0 escapes, the coordinate can be continued only down to the equipotential through 0, a curve shaped like a figure eight. Each of its two loops encloses part of the filled Julia set, and inside each loop the same splitting happens again at the preimages of 0. This endless splitting is why the Julia set is then a Cantor set, as the Julia chapter describes.

External rays and angle doubling

When Kc is connected, the external ray at angle θ is the curve of all points with external angle θ: the image under φc−1 of the radial line {re2πiθ : r > 1}. Angles are measured in whole turns, from 0 to 1. Rays and equipotentials cross at right angles, as radii and circles do, because φc preserves angles.

Because the coordinate turns the map into squaring, and squaring doubles arguments, fc carries the ray at angle θ onto the ray at angle 2θ, counted modulo 1 (Milnor’s lectures, §18). In binary notation, doubling shifts the digits one place to the left and drops the digit that crosses the point: .0110… becomes .110… . Angle doubling is therefore a complete record of how the map moves the rays.

Rational angles are exactly the angles that are eventually periodic under doubling. Those with an odd denominator are periodic, such as 1/3 → 2/3 → 1/3; those with an even denominator are strictly preperiodic, such as 1/6 → 1/3 → 2/3. When Kc is connected, every rational ray lands: as the potential decreases to zero, it ends at a single point of the Julia set (Milnor’s lectures, §18).

  • Every periodic ray lands at a periodic point, which is repelling or parabolic (Sullivan, Douady and Hubbard; Theorem 18.1).
  • Every rational ray that is not periodic lands at a point that is preperiodic but not periodic (Corollary 18.4).
  • Conversely, every repelling or parabolic periodic point is the landing point of at least one ray, and that ray is periodic (Douady and Yoccoz; Theorem 18.2).

Several rays can land at the same point. For c = −2 everything can be computed: the ray at angle θ is the image of a radial line under w ↦ w + 1/w, so it lands at 2 cos 2πθ. The ray at angle 0 lands at the fixed point 2, and the rays at 1/3 and 2/3 both land at the other fixed point, −1, one from above and one from below. Doubling swaps them, while their landing point stays fixed: near −1 the map exchanges the two sides of the segment. The ray at 1/2 lands at −2, the critical value. For Douady’s rabbit, the rays at 1/7, 2/7 and 4/7 land together at the fixed point α and turn around it by one third at each step, as the periodicity chapter describes.

The same coordinates for the Mandelbrot set

Adrien Douady and John Hubbard carried these coordinates over to the parameter plane with one idea: give each parameter outside M the coordinate of its own critical value. For such a c the orbit of 0 escapes, so φc reaches only down to the equipotential through 0. But the critical value c = fc(0) has twice the potential of 0 and lies above that level, so φc(c) is defined, and they set

ΦM(c) = φc(c).

They proved that ΦM maps the outside of M one-to-one onto the outside of the closed unit disk, preserving angles (Douady and Hubbard’s Orsay notes, Theorem 8.1, first announced in 1982). Three consequences follow:

  • M is connected (Corollary 8.3); the Mandelbrot chapter explains why.
  • The potential of M at c equals the potential of the critical value in its own dynamical plane: GM(c) = Gc(c) (Corollary 8.4). So the colored bands of an escape-time picture of M approximate the equipotentials of M, just as they do for a Julia set.
  • The external angle of c in the parameter plane equals the external angle of the critical value c in the dynamical plane of fc (Corollary 8.4).

The parameter ray at angle θ is the curve of parameters outside M with external angle θ. Parameter rays and dynamical rays live in different planes, but the last identity links them: c lies on the parameter ray at angle θ exactly when its critical value has external angle θ in its own dynamical plane. No map acts on the parameter plane, so there is no doubling there; angles are addresses, and their meaning comes from the dynamical planes. Douady summed up the method as “You first plow in the dynamical plane and then harvest in the parameter plane” (quoted by Lyubich).

Where parameter rays land

A ray lands when it approaches one well-defined boundary point as the potential decreases to zero. Every rational parameter ray lands: periodic angles land at parabolic parameters, and strictly preperiodic angles land at Misiurewicz parameters (Orsay notes, Theorem 13.1; Eberlein, Mukherjee and Schleicher, Theorem 1.1). At the root of every hyperbolic component other than the main cardioid exactly two rays land, both with the component’s period; at the cusp 1/4 only the ray at angle 0 lands (Milnor, Periodic orbits, external rays and the Mandelbrot set, Corollary 5.4, for the rays at periodic angles; Lyubich’s book, Theorem 47.15, for all rays). Conversely, every angle of period n ≥ 2 belongs to the root of exactly one component of period n (Corollary 5.5). So the angles of exact period n pair off, two at each root: Lavaurs’ rule finds the partner of every periodic angle other than 0.

Their parameter angles are exactly the angles of the dynamical rays that land at the critical value in their own Julia set (Orsay notes, Theorem 8.2; Eberlein, Mukherjee and Schleicher, Theorem 1.1). The angles of the bulbs

Which two rays land at the root of the p/q bulb on the main cardioid? Their angles can be read off a wheel of dynamical rays. For every c in the bulb, q rays land at the fixed point α, and the map turns them like a wheel, as the periodicity chapter describes. In terms of angles: list the q angles in increasing order; doubling moves each of them p places on in this list, going round from the end to the beginning. The p-th and the (p + 1)-th angle of the list, θ− and θ+, bound the narrowest gap between the rays at α, and they are the angles of the two parameter rays at the root. Milnor’s theorems on orbit portraits (Milnor, Theorems 1.1 and 1.2), described in the next section, explain why.

The binary digits of all q angles follow from the wheel alone. The first digit of an angle is 0 in the upper half of the circle, from 0 to 1/2, and 1 in the lower half, from 1/2 to 1. Doubling shifts the digits one place to the left, so the k-th digit tells in which half the angle lies after k − 1 doublings. The lower half holds exactly the last p angles of the list. Doubling makes an angle in the upper half larger, since 2θ > θ, and an angle in the lower half smaller, since 2θ − 1 < θ. Moving p places on makes the first q − p angles larger and the last p smaller, because these go round to the beginning.

So the digits depend only on the order of the angles, not on where exactly they lie, and q evenly spaced points give the same digits. Walk around the points 0, 1⁠/⁠q, …, (q − 1)⁠/⁠q in steps of p⁠/⁠q, and write 1 at each of the last p points, those from 1 − p/q on, and 0 at the others. The point k/q stands for the (k + 1)-th angle of the list, so starting at p/q gives the repeating block of θ+, and starting one point earlier, at (p − 1)⁠/⁠q, the block of θ−. As a formula: for k = 1, 2, …, q, the k-th digit of θ+ is 1 when the fractional part of kp/q is at least 1 − p/q, and 0 otherwise. The two blocks differ only at the end, where θ+ has 10 and θ− has 01. They are the rotation words of the slope p/q, and Douady and Hubbard stated an equivalent formula in their 1982 note (§6).

For the 2/5 bulb, the five angles are 5, 9, 10, 18 and 20 thirty-firsts, and doubling moves each two places on: 10/31 → 20/31 → 9/31 → 18/31 → 5/31 → 10/31. The last two, 18/31 and 20/31, lie in the lower half. On the evenly spaced points, the walk from 2/5 visits 2/5, 4/5, 1/5, 3/5 and 0, and the last two points are 3/5 and 4/5. Both walks give the digits 0, 1, 0, 1, 0, so θ+ = .⁠(01010) = 10/31, and starting one place earlier gives θ− = .⁠(01001) = 9/31. The narrowest gap, between them, is 1/31 of a turn.

The roots come from the cardioid formula c = λ/2 − λ2/4 with λ = e2πip/q, and the bulb (q − p)/q is the mirror image, with angles 1 − θ. The internal angle 1/3, the external angles 1/7 and 2/7, and the direction of the root as seen from 0 are three different numbers; the ray at angle 1/3 lands at −3/4, not on the 1/3 bulb.

Since θ+ and θ− differ only in their last two repeating digits, their difference is exactly 1/(2q − 1), the angular width of the wake of the bulb. There are φ(q) bulbs of period q, one for each p prime to q, and the widths of all their wakes add up to exactly 1:

∑q ≥ 2 φ(q) / (2q − 1) = 1/3 + 2/7 + 2/15 + 4/31 + … = 1.

This follows from the identity ∑ φ(n)xn/(1 − xn) = x/(1 − x)2 at x = 1/2, after removing the term for n = 1. So the limbs of the main cardioid take up all angles except a set of total length zero; the angles left over belong to the points of the cardioid itself between the bulbs (Douady and Hubbard, 1982, §6).

Wakes, limbs and orbit portraits

The two rays landing at the root of a component, together with their landing point, cut the plane into two parts. The part that does not contain the main cardioid is the wake of the component, and the part of M in the closed wake is its limb. The angles of the rays inside the wake form the interval between the two angles of the pair; for the 1/3 bulb, from 1/7 to 2/7.

Milnor’s theorem on orbit portraits explains what the wake means for the dynamics. The orbit portrait of a periodic orbit records, for each of its points, the angles of the rays that land there. For every c in the 1/3 wake, the rays at 1/7, 2/7 and 4/7 land at the fixed point α, which is repelling: the portrait is {1/7, 2/7, 4/7}. The three rays cut the plane into three sectors, and the critical value c lies in the narrowest one, between 1/7 and 2/7 (Milnor’s paper, Theorem 1.1): exactly the angles of the two parameter rays that bound the wake. Milnor’s Theorem 1.2 makes this general. For each portrait with at least two rays per point, a quadratic map has a repelling orbit with that portrait exactly when c lies in the corresponding wake, and a parabolic orbit with that portrait exactly when c is its root.

Two different wakes are either disjoint or one lies inside the other (Corollary 1.3, “orbit forcing”), so the wakes and limbs form a tree. Milnor’s worked example is the period-six bulb attached at the 1/3 point of the period-two disk, at c = −9/8 + (√3/8)i. Its orbit portrait has two points, with the rays at 22/63, 25/63 and 37/63 landing at one and those at 11/63, 44/63 and 50/63 at the other, and its wake lies between 22/63 and 25/63. These are the angles that tuning produces from 1/7 and 2/7.

How rays are drawn

A parameter c lies on the ray at angle θ, at the level where |⁠ΦM(c)| = r, when φc(c) = re2πiθ. Since φc turns the map into squaring and is close to the identity far out, this means that the point fcn(c) of the critical orbit is close to (re2πiθ)2n once that number is large. So a ray can be followed inward from far out: lower r a little at a time, take n large enough, and correct c with a few steps of Newton’s method, using the derivative of fcn(c) with respect to c. Dynamical rays are traced in the same way, with z in place of c. The figure of the rabbit and the Mandelbrot set and the ray explorer are drawn like this. Rays approach a Misiurewicz point quickly, by a roughly constant factor with each halving of the potential, while near a parabolic root they are slow: doubling the number of steps only halves the remaining distance at the root of a bulb, and only quarters it at a cusp such as 1/4.

Irrational angles

Rational rays all land. Of the irrational ones, almost every ray lands, by a general theorem about conformal maps: the map from the outside of the disk to the outside of M has a radial limit in almost every direction (Fatou’s theorem). Whether every ray lands, with a landing point that depends continuously on the angle, is equivalent to the local connectivity of M, the MLC conjecture, which is open (what remains open). If it holds, M is a closed disk pinched along chords: each chord joining two angles whose rays land at the same point is squeezed to that point. This is the “pinched disk” model of Douady and Thurston.

Key ideas

  • The escape potential Gc measures how fast points escape; it is zero exactly on the filled Julia set, and its level curves are the equipotentials.
  • Böttcher’s coordinate φc turns z2 + c into squaring near infinity; when the orbit of 0 is bounded it covers the whole outside of Kc.
  • The map carries the external ray at angle θ to the ray at 2θ, so angle doubling records the dynamics; rational rays land at periodic or preperiodic points.
  • Douady and Hubbard’s ΦM(c) = φc(c) gives the outside of M the same coordinates; periodic parameter rays land in pairs at roots of components (the ray at 0 alone at the cusp 1/4), preperiodic ones at Misiurewicz points.
  • The two rays at the root of a bulb bound its wake; for c in the wake the corresponding dynamical rays land together on a repelling orbit with a fixed orbit portrait.

Check yourself

  1. For c = −2, which rays land at 0? Where do their images land?

    The ray at θ lands at 2 cos 2πθ, which is 0 for θ = 1/4 and 3/4. Doubling takes both to 1/2, and the ray at 1/2 lands at −2 = f(0), as it must.

  2. Use the rule to find the two rays at the root of the 1/4 bulb.

    Here p = 1, so only the last of the points 0, 1/4, 1/2 and 3/4 gives a 1. The walk from 1/4 in steps of 1/4 visits 1/4, 1/2, 3/4 and 0, so θ+ = .⁠(0010) = 2/15. The walk from 0 visits 0, 1/4, 1/2 and 3/4, so θ− = .⁠(0001) = 1/15. Their difference is 1/15 = 1/(24 − 1).

  3. The rays at 7/15 and 8/15 land at the same point. Why must that point be real?

    The Mandelbrot set is symmetric under complex conjugation, which takes the ray at angle θ to the ray at angle 1 − θ. So the landing point of the ray at 8/15 is the conjugate of the landing point of the ray at 7/15. If the two points are the same, it equals its own conjugate and is real: it is the root of the real period-four copy near −1.94.

  4. For c = −1, the basilica, which rays land at the fixed point α = (1 − √5)/2?

    The rays at 1/3 and 2/3. The basilica lies in the wake of the 1/2 bulb, where the two rays of the rotation cycle 1/3 → 2/3 → 1/3 land at α and are swapped by the map, a rotation by one half.

  5. Is the angle 5/12 periodic or preperiodic under doubling? What does that say about where its parameter ray lands?

    5/12 → 5/6 → 2/3 → 1/3 → 2/3: preperiod 2, period 2. A strictly preperiodic angle lands at a Misiurewicz point, here ≈ −1.5437.

  6. How wide is the wake of the 2/5 bulb, and which bulbs on the main cardioid have wakes wider than 1/10?

    1/31. A wake of period q has width 1/(2q − 1), which exceeds 1/10 only for q = 2 and 3: the 1/2 bulb (1/3) and the 1/3 and 2/3 bulbs (1/7 each).
