Orbits: what iteration actually does

Level: IntroductoryBuilds on Mathematical foundations

A fractal picture compresses infinitely many journeys into one image. An orbit is one of those journeys: the successive positions of a starting point as the same rule is applied again and again.

What an orbit is

The foundations chapter set up the rule fc(z) = z2 + c. Fix the parameter c, choose a starting point z0, and apply the rule again and again: the points z0, z1, z2, … form the forward orbit of z0. An orbit is a sequence of separate points. Lines drawn between them in a figure only guide the eye; an orbit may jump, circle, spiral or scatter.

What an orbit does depends on both choices, c and z0. Most of this chapter follows the orbit of the critical point 0, which decides membership in the Mandelbrot set. The last sections look at other starting points, the question behind the Julia sets.

Four exact critical orbits

Starting from zero gives examples whose behavior can be proved with elementary arithmetic. They return throughout the atlas, so they are worth knowing by heart.

Parameter Orbit of 0 What it demonstrates
c = 0 0 → 0 → 0 → … A fixed point: period 1.
c = −1 0 → −1 → 0 → −1 → … A cycle of exact period 2.
c = −2 0 → −2 → 2 → 2 → … Two steps before a fixed point: preperiod 2, eventual period 1.
c = 1 0 → 1 → 2 → 5 → 26 → 677 → … Escape to infinity; this parameter lies outside the Mandelbrot set.

Period, preperiod, and merely approaching a cycle

A point a is periodic if fcp(a) = a for some positive integer p. Its exact period, also called its least or primitive period, is the smallest such integer. A fixed point is therefore a period-one point. A period-two point also returns after four or six steps, but its exact period remains two.

A cycle is the finite collection of points visited by a periodic orbit. Beginning at another point of the same cycle changes the starting label, not the cycle. If an orbit reaches a cycle after exactly m steps, where m is as small as possible, it has preperiod m. A strictly preperiodic point has m > 0; periodic points themselves have preperiod zero.

Approaching a cycle is different from landing on it. For c = −0.1, the critical orbit begins 0, −0.1, −0.09, −0.0919, … and tends to the fixed point (1 − √1.4)/2 ≈ −0.091608, but no entry ever equals that limit. Rounded decimal output can make such an orbit look exactly periodic.

This distinction explains a common shorthand. A bulb of the Mandelbrot set called “period five” consists of parameters whose map has an attracting cycle of period five; the orbit of zero approaches that cycle. It repeats exactly after five steps only at one parameter of the bulb, its center.

Why some cycles attract and others repel

Suppose a0, …, ap−1 form a cycle of period p. Applying fc p times brings a0 back to itself, and a point near a0 back near a0. These p steps, taken together as a single step, form the cycle’s return map fcp, and a0 is a fixed point of it. The derivative of the return map at a0 is the multiplier; by the chain rule it is the product of the derivatives 2aj along the cycle:

λ = (fcp)′(a0) = ∏j=0p−1 2aj.

For a small displacement ε, the return map has the expansion fcp(a0 + ε) = a0 + λε + a remainder O(|ε|2) containing quadratic and higher powers. Thus |λ| describes the first-order change in distance per complete return, while the argument of λ describes a rotation. For a fixed point, p = 1 and λ = 2a0, as in the foundations chapter.

Multiplier Name Local behavior
λ = 0 Superattracting The linear term vanishes; nearby errors shrink at a higher-order rate.
0 < |λ| < 1 Attracting Nearby points approach the cycle under repeated returns.
|λ| > 1 Repelling Small displacements grow locally under returns.
|λ| = 1 Neutral or indifferent The linear term alone cannot decide the dynamics.

A cycle is neutral when |λ| = 1, that is, when its multiplier lies on the unit circle. Multiplying by such a λ only turns a small displacement, without stretching or shrinking it: λ = e2πiθ, where θ is the angle of the turn, measured in turns. If θ is a fraction with denominator q, then λq = 1 (λ is a root of unity), and the cycle is parabolic. After q returns the turns add up to whole turns, so the first-order term leaves nearby points where they are, and the small remainder decides. Along some directions nearby points are drawn in, along others pushed away; the cusp c = 1/4 below is an example. If θ is irrational, the cycle is either a Siegel cycle or a Cremer cycle. Near a Siegel cycle, a holomorphic change of coordinates turns the return map into an exact rotation by θ (the map is linearizable), so nearby points circle the cycle forever. Near a Cremer cycle no such coordinates exist, and other cycles and even points whose orbits escape lie arbitrarily close to it. Which of the two occurs depends on how well fractions approximate θ. The chapter on neutral cycles explains all three cases; Milnor’s lectures develop the local theory.

For the critical two-cycle at c = −1, λ = (2·0)(2·−1) = 0, so it is superattracting. Its return map is fc2(z) = z4 − 2z2, whose derivative 4z3 − 4z is also 0 at 0. At c = −2, the fixed point 2 has λ = 4 and is repelling, yet the critical orbit lands on it exactly. Repulsion describes what nearby points do: an exceptional orbit can still arrive precisely at a repelling point.

How fast an orbit settles

The multiplier also tells how an orbit approaches an attracting cycle, and what it looks like on the way.

  • Attracting, 0 < |λ| < 1. Each return multiplies the distance to the cycle by about |λ| and turns it by the argument of λ. For c = −0.1 the fixed point has λ = 2a ≈ −0.1832. The orbit of 0 lands alternately on either side of it, and each error is about −0.18 times the previous one: −0.0084, then 0.0016, then −0.00029. After seven steps it is within 10−6. Kœnigs proved in 1884 that near such a point there is a change of coordinates in which the map is exactly multiplication by λ (Milnor’s lectures, Theorem 6.1). Orbits near an attracting point therefore look like geometric sequences: straight lines into the point when λ is positive, zigzags when it is negative, and spirals when it is not real.
  • Superattracting, λ = 0. The error is roughly squared at each return, so the number of correct digits about doubles. For c = 0, starting at 0.1 gives 0.01, 0.0001, 10−8. Böttcher’s theorem says that near such a point a change of coordinates turns the map into w ↦ w2 exactly (Theorem 6.7 in the same lectures). The same theorem, applied at infinity, gives the coordinates used for external rays.
  • Neutral, |λ| = 1. Convergence, when it happens, is slow. At the cusp c = 1/4 the critical orbit approaches the fixed point 1/2 with an error of about 1⁠/⁠n after n steps: the special-points chapter shows that the error obeys en+1 = en − en2, so 1⁠/⁠en grows by about 1 per step.
  • Repelling, |λ| > 1. Small errors grow by about |λ| per return. Near the fixed point 2 at c = −2, an error of 10−16, the size of a rounding error, is multiplied by about 4 at each step and reaches size 1 after about 27 steps.

Why the orbit of zero is special

The orbit of the critical point 0, namely 0, c, c2 + c, …, is decisive for two reasons. First, the filled Julia set is connected exactly when this orbit remains bounded. Second, a quadratic polynomial has at most one finite cycle that is not repelling, and when that cycle is attracting or parabolic, the critical orbit is attracted to it. In particular there is at most one finite attracting cycle, while the same map has infinitely many repelling cycles. Devaney’s explanation of the critical orbit states the connectedness criterion and the attracting case. Milnor’s lectures prove that every attracting or parabolic cycle attracts a critical point (Lemmas 10.2–10.3), and Lyubich’s book that there is at most one non-repelling cycle (Theorem 28.13).

These theorems are the bridge between the two kinds of picture. To find the attracting cycle of a map, if it has one, follow the orbit of 0. Testing that one orbit across the parameter plane organizes entire Julia sets into one Mandelbrot map. Other starting points of the same map can still behave differently, as the next section shows.

Basins, boundaries, and different starting points

The basin of attraction of a cycle consists of starting points whose orbits approach that cycle. The immediate basin consists of the basin components containing its cycle points; other pieces of the basin feed into these after several iterations. The basin of infinity consists of starting points whose orbits escape.

The map f0(z) = z2 is an exact laboratory, since zn = z02n. Starting at 1/2 gives 1/2, 1/4, 1/16, 1/256, …, attracted to the fixed point 0. Starting at 2 gives 2, 4, 16, 256, …, which escapes. Starting at i gives i, −1, 1, 1, …, which lands on the repelling fixed point 1. A point on the unit circle at an irrational fraction of a full turn never becomes periodic, although it stays on the circle forever. The basin of 0 is the open unit disk, the basin of infinity is the outside of the circle, and the circle itself separates them: it is the Julia set of this map, as the Julia chapter explains.

Orbits on a computer

An orbit computed exactly, or with its rounding errors bounded, can prove escape: once a single iterate is farther from 0 than max(2, |c|), the orbit escapes, by the argument in the foundations chapter. The reverse is not true. An orbit that is still inside the radius after a million steps may escape later, especially near a parabolic parameter, where orbits linger for a long time.

Rounding is the second limitation. A computer stores about 16 significant digits, and every step rounds. Near repelling cycles, as seen above, such errors grow geometrically, so after a few dozen steps a computed orbit can follow a different path from the true one. The chapter on chaos shows the same effect for x ↦ x2 − 2, where, measured as an angle, errors double at each step. Exact equations, such as the ones in the table above, or rigorous error bounds are needed to certify delicate claims; a computed orbit is the way to discover them. The chapter on computing the pictures shows how bounded errors turn pictures into proofs.

Key ideas

  • An orbit is the sequence of points z0, z1, z2, … obtained by applying fc again and again.
  • A periodic point returns to itself; its exact period is the smallest number of steps. A preperiodic point lands on a cycle after some steps. Approaching a cycle is different from landing on it.
  • The multiplier of a cycle, the product of the numbers 2aj along it, classifies the cycle as superattracting, attracting, neutral or repelling.
  • Near an attracting cycle, errors shrink by a fixed factor per return; near a superattracting one, the number of correct digits roughly doubles; near a repelling one, errors grow by a fixed factor.
  • A quadratic polynomial has at most one non-repelling cycle, and the orbit of 0 finds it when it is attracting or parabolic. That is why one orbit can organize the whole parameter plane.

Check yourself

  1. Find the exact period and the preperiod of the orbit of 0 for c = −2 and for c = i.

    For c = −2 the orbit is 0, −2, 2, 2, …: it reaches the fixed point 2 after two steps, so the preperiod is 2 and the period is 1. For c = i it is 0, i, −1 + i, −i, −1 + i, …: it reaches the cycle {−1 + i, −i} after two steps, so the preperiod is 2 and the period is 2.

  2. For c = −1.1 the points a ≈ 0.0916 and b ≈ −1.0916 form a cycle of period 2. Is it attracting? Check that their product is c + 1.

    The multiplier is (2a)(2b) = 4ab. The product is ab ≈ −0.1 = c + 1, so λ ≈ −0.4. Since |λ| < 1 the cycle attracts, and the orbit of 0, which begins 0, −1.1, 0.11, −1.0879, 0.0835, …, approaches it. The periodicity chapter shows that the product is exactly c + 1 for every c.

  3. For c = −0.1 the critical orbit approaches a fixed point with multiplier λ ≈ −0.183, starting at distance about 0.09. Roughly how many steps does it take to come within 10−6?

    Each step multiplies the error by about 0.183, so we need 0.09 · 0.183n < 10−6, that is n > log(1.1 · 10−5)/log(0.183) ≈ 6.7. The actual errors, computed in the section above, are about 1.8 · 10−6 after 6 steps and 3.3 · 10−7 after 7 (the first step shrinks the error more than the estimate assumes): seven steps.

  4. Under z ↦ z2, start on the unit circle at one third of a full turn. What is the orbit, and is it attracting or repelling?

    Squaring doubles the angle: 1/3 of a turn goes to 2/3, and 2/3 goes to 4/3, which is 1/3 again. So the orbit is a cycle of period 2. Its multiplier is (2z1)(2z2) = 4z1z2, and z1z2 is the point at 1/3 + 2/3 = 1 turn, which is 1. So λ = 4: the cycle repels, even though its points stay on the circle forever.

  5. Why can z2 + c never have two different attracting cycles?

    Every attracting cycle attracts the orbit of the critical point 0. A single orbit cannot converge to two different cycles, so there is at most one.

  6. A computer reports that the orbit of 0 for some c is still within distance 2 of the origin after 10,000 steps. What can you conclude?

    Nothing definite. The orbit may be bounded, in which case c is in the Mandelbrot set, or it may escape later, for example near a parabolic parameter. Rounding errors may also have changed its course. Only a proof, such as finding the attracting cycle exactly, settles the question.
