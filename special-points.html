Landmarks on the boundary

Misiurewicz points, cusps & the Feigenbaum point

Level: IntermediateBuilds on External rays & angles

A boundary point can mark a cycle becoming neutral, a critical orbit landing exactly on a repelling cycle, or an infinite cascade of returns. These mechanisms produce different landmarks, even when a picture makes them look similar.

Four kinds of landmark

The parameter c selects the map fc(z) = z2 + c. Its distinguished orbit begins at the critical point 0. Following that orbit gives a more reliable classification than the outline of a rendered image.

Landmark Critical-orbit behavior Example
Center 0 itself lies on a periodic orbit. The cycle is superattracting, with multiplier zero. c = −1: 0 → −1 → 0.
Parabolic parameter The critical orbit is attracted toward a neutral cycle with a root-of-unity multiplier; it never lands on that cycle exactly. The cusp c = 1/4.
Misiurewicz parameter 0 is not periodic but reaches a repelling periodic cycle after finitely many steps. c = −2 or c = i.
Period-doubling limit Successive return periods grow without bound. No finite critical cycle describes the limiting orbit. The real Feigenbaum parameter, approximately −1.401155.

A center lies inside a hyperbolic component; the other three kinds lie on the boundary of the Mandelbrot set. “Center” is a dynamical notion: a point at the visual middle of a spiral is usually not one.

Misiurewicz points: eventually repeating, but repelling

A Misiurewicz parameter has a critical orbit that is strictly preperiodic: an initial nonrepeating segment leads into a cycle, while 0 itself never returns to 0. If the orbit first enters a cycle after m steps and that cycle has least period p, then

fcm+p(0) = fcm(0),   with m > 0 and minimal m, p.

The eventual cycle is repelling for these quadratic maps. An exactly chosen critical orbit lands on it, but a small perturbation generally does not. This combination of exact landing and instability makes such parameters useful landmarks. Tan Lei’s paper on similarity states the definition, introduces c = i and c = −2 as its first examples, and works through c = i in detail.

The word “preperiodic” sometimes includes points already on a cycle. The qualifier strictly rules those out. This prevents confusing a Misiurewicz parameter with a center. Preperiod also depends on whether an author starts counting at the critical point 0 or the critical value c. This page counts from 0 throughout.

Example 1: the left tip, c = −2

0 → −2 → 2 → 2 → 2 → …

Two steps bring the critical orbit to the fixed point 2, so the preperiod is 2 and the eventual period is 1. The orbit arrives there from −2, and this is no accident: the only points that the map sends to a fixed point a are a itself and its mirror point −a, so an orbit that lands on a fixed point passes through the mirror point one step before. The derivative is f′(z) = 2z; at 2 it equals 4. A displacement from 2 is therefore expanded by approximately a factor of four per iteration while it remains small.

This example is also unusually explicit in the dynamical plane. Put z = 2 cos t. The double-angle identity gives z2 − 2 = 2 cos(2t). Hence the real interval [−2, 2] maps into itself. In fact the Julia set for this parameter is exactly that interval. The Mandelbrot set contains infinitely many small copies along the real axis near −2, yet under high magnification at −2 it looks more and more like a straight segment ending there, just like this Julia set; local resemblance below explains why.

Example 2: c = i

0 → i → −1 + i → −i → −1 + i → −i → …

Check the last two steps directly: (−1 + i)2 + i = −i, and (−i)2 + i = −1 + i. The preperiod from 0 is 2 and the cycle period is 2. Its multiplier is the product of derivatives around the cycle:

λ = 2(−1 + i) · 2(−i) = 4 + 4i,   |⁠λ| = 4√2 > 1.

The multiplier combines expansion with rotation. Its modulus 4√2 ≈ 5.66 is the local expansion per two-step return, and its argument, 45°, is the local turn. These two numbers reappear in the parameter plane: near c = i, magnifying the Mandelbrot set by 4√2 and turning it by 45° reproduces its picture more and more exactly, as Tan Lei showed for this very example (local resemblance).

Tips, branch points and spirals

What the Mandelbrot set looks like near a Misiurewicz parameter c is decided by the cycle that the orbit of 0 lands on. First, as many arms of M meet at c as parameter rays land there, and these are as many as the dynamical rays that land at the critical value c in its own Julia set (where parameter rays land). Since 0 is the only critical point, the map carries the rays that land at any other point one-to-one onto the rays that land at its image, so c has as many rays as the point of the cycle where its orbit arrives. One ray makes c a tip, the end of an arm: at −2 the orbit lands on the fixed point β = 2, where only the ray at angle 0 lands, and at i on a two-cycle with one ray at each point. Two rays make c a point where two arms join, and three or more a branch point, as where three or five branches meet in the 1/3 and the 2/5 limb, at points whose orbits land on the fixed point α. The Julia sets themselves show the same two shapes (centers of Julia sets): β sits at an outer tip, and arms meet at α.

These two branch points follow a rule. Every limb of the main cardioid, the p/q limb, has a main branch point, also called its principal Misiurewicz point: after q steps the orbit of 0 arrives at −α, the other preimage of α, and after q + 1 steps at α itself (Branner and Fagella, 1999). For the parameters of the p/q limb, q rays land at α, so q parameter rays land at the main branch point, and q branches of the limb meet there: the spokes of its main antenna. Robert Devaney, who calls the point the main junction point, uses this to read off the period of a bulb on the main cardioid by eye: count the spokes of its main antenna, including the one that leads back to the bulb (Devaney’s periods of the bulbs). In the 1/2 limb the main branch point is −1.5436890127 on the real axis, where only two branches join, so that for q = 2 it is a branch point in name only; in the 1/4 limb it is c ≈ 0.3663629834 + 0.5915337733i, with four, and in the 2/7 limb c ≈ 0.1435562145 + 0.6520726473i, with seven.

Second, the multiplier λ of the cycle decides how the picture repeats: magnifying by |⁠λ| around c and turning by the angle of λ reproduces it more and more exactly (local resemblance). Each such step carries the arms onto one another, and with q arms every q-th step carries each arm onto itself. If those q steps add up to almost no turn, the arms run nearly straight, as at the branch points in the menu below and at −2, where λ = 4 does not turn at all and the set looks more and more like a straight segment. If they turn the picture far for little magnification, the arms wind into spirals. That happens near parabolic parameters, where the multiplier of the cycle is close to modulus 1: in seahorse valley, near −3/4, α has a multiplier close to −1.

Tips deserve a closer look. Along an arm that ends in a Misiurewicz tip, the decorations repeat, smaller each time by the factor |⁠λ|. The menu’s last example is such a tip in seahorse valley, where an arm of the double spiral in its fifth example ends: its orbit lands after 22 steps on β, whose multiplier λ has modulus 3.03 and an angle of only −2.6°. The arm carries smaller and smaller copies of the double spiral, Misiurewicz points whose orbits land on α after 24, 25, 26, … steps, the first of them being that double spiral itself. The closer a copy lies to the tip, the longer its orbit stays near β before it moves on to α, one step more for each copy, and each step near β multiplies small distances by about 3.03. So the copies approach the tip by that factor, turned by about 2.6° each time, and the chain of copies runs almost straight into it. The factor is close to 3 for a reason: the two fixed points add up to α + β = 1, so their multipliers 2α and 2β add up to 2, and near −3/4, where the multiplier of α is close to −1, that of β is close to 3.

A single ray is not enough to make a Misiurewicz point. Saeed Zakeri proved that a typical point of the boundary of M, in the sense of harmonic measure, is the landing point of only one parameter ray (Zakeri, 2006). Harmonic measure gives no weight to single points, and Misiurewicz points are only countably many, so almost none of these points are Misiurewicz points: their orbit of 0 never lands on a cycle, and no multiplier of a cycle makes the picture around them repeat at a fixed factor. Not all of them are tips either: the points of the main cardioid’s edge at irrational internal angles have a single ray too. A tip whose decorations do repeat at a fixed factor as one zooms in is usually a Misiurewicz point, and then the factor is |⁠λ|.

What, then, is a typical point of the boundary like? Jacek Graczyk and Grzegorz Świątek proved that at almost every point of the boundary, again in the sense of harmonic measure, the map is Collet–Eckmann: the derivative along the orbit of the critical value grows exponentially, |⁠(fcn)′(c)| ≥ Ceγn for all n, with constants C, γ > 0, so that a tiny displacement of the critical value is stretched exponentially along its orbit, as long as it stays tiny (Graczyk and Świątek, 2000). In this a typical point resembles a Misiurewicz point, where the derivative grows by the factor |⁠λ| with every turn around the cycle, but its orbit of 0 never lands on a cycle. Stanislav Smirnov showed in the same year that the exceptions are rarer still: the parameter rays land at Collet–Eckmann parameters for all angles outside a set of Hausdorff dimension 0 (Smirnov, 2000). The Julia sets of the typical parameters have Hausdorff dimension less than 2 (Graczyk and Świątek), unlike those of a dense, generic set of parameters on the boundary, which have dimension 2 (how rough the boundary is).

Parabolic points and cusps

A periodic cycle is parabolic when its multiplier is a root of unity. After enough returns, the multiplier becomes 1. Linearization alone then cannot tell whether nearby points move in or out; higher-order terms create attracting and repelling directions, often described as petals. The chapter on neutral cycles describes these petals and the irrationally neutral cases.

At c = 1/4, the fixed-point equation z2 + 1/4 = z becomes (z − 1/2)2 = 0. The fixed point is 1/2 and its multiplier is 1. Starting at 0, the orbit increases toward 1/2 without reaching it in a finite number of steps. Write u = z − 1/2; the local map is

u ↦ u + u2.

The correction is quadratic rather than a uniform linear contraction. If the real error is en = 1/2 − zn, then en+1 = en − en2. Its decrease becomes very slow. This provides a concrete reason why orbits near a parabolic parameter can linger for many iterations.

The parameter is also the cusp of the main cardioid: in the parameterization c = λ/2 − λ2/4 of the periodicity chapter, the derivative vanishes at λ = 1, and the boundary turns back on itself there.

At c = −3/4, the fixed point −1/2 instead has multiplier −1. It is parabolic in the root-of-unity sense, and its two-step multiplier is 1. This is the attachment of the period-two bulb to the main cardioid, not the main cardioid’s cusp. The critical orbit approaches the neutral fixed point with alternating behavior. Both 1/4 and −3/4 are parabolic parameters, but their geometric and bifurcation roles differ.

Addresses by external rays

Most landmarks in this chapter can also be found from outside, along the external rays that come in from infinity at fixed angles. One ray lands at the cusp 1/4, at angle 0, one at −2, at angle 1/2, and one at i, at angle 1/6; two rays land at −3/4, at 1/3 and 2/3, and two at the root of every other component; Misiurewicz points can have one, two or more. The rays chapter explains where rays land and what their angles say about the dynamics.

The Feigenbaum accumulation point

Following the real period-doubling chain encounters attracting cycles of periods 1, 2, 4, 8, and so on. Their bifurcations accumulate at approximately c = −1.401155. The limiting map is infinitely renormalizable: one can repeatedly take an appropriate return map at smaller scales. Its critical orbit is not a finite preperiodic orbit, so this point is not Misiurewicz. McMullen’s Feigenbaum example (§7.4) describes this structure. Whether the Mandelbrot set is locally connected at this point was settled only recently: it is, by a theorem of Dzmitry Dudko and Mikhail Lyubich (2026).

The coordinate −1.401155… belongs to the family z2 + c. The logistic map, which is the same family in other coordinates, reaches the same point at r ≈ 3.5699; genuinely different families end their cascades elsewhere, the sine map x ↦ r sin(πx) near r ≈ 0.8656. What all of them share is the Feigenbaum constant δ ≈ 4.6692, the limiting ratio of successive doubling intervals, described in the minibrots chapter.

Landmarks are everywhere on the boundary

The special points are not rare. Every point of the boundary of the Mandelbrot set is a limit of Misiurewicz parameters, a limit of parabolic parameters, and a limit of centers of hyperbolic components; conversely, centers accumulate only at points of the boundary (Lyubich’s book, Proposition 36.4). So however far one zooms into the boundary, small hyperbolic components, with their cusps and attachment points, and Misiurewicz points keep appearing. They are countably many points, yet they are found arbitrarily close to every boundary point.

Local resemblance and what remains delicate

At a Misiurewicz parameter, the Mandelbrot set near that parameter and the corresponding Julia set near its critical value have asymptotically matching geometry after suitable magnification and rotation (Tan Lei, 1990). The magnification and turn under which the picture repeats come from the multiplier of the repelling cycle: for c = i it is 4 + 4i, so magnifying by 4√2 ≈ 5.66 and turning by 45° maps the picture near i approximately onto itself, ever more exactly as one zooms in. Zooming by 4√2 without turning, the picture comes back to its original orientation after eight steps. The Julia set near its critical value i repeats itself in the same way, and Tan Lei proved more: in the limit the two pictures differ only by one fixed scale and rotation, for c = i a factor of about 1.12 and a turn of about 27°. The resemblance is local: it compares small neighborhoods in two different planes. Kawahira’s treatment of Tan Lei’s theorem gives a simpler proof. At the tip −2 the Julia set is the segment [−2, 2], so near −2 the Mandelbrot set, too, looks more and more like a straight segment. The zoom viewer opens on the neighborhood of i.

To identify a landmark numerically, solve its orbit relation exactly, check that the preperiod and period are minimal, and compute the multiplier of the cycle. A long list of nearly repeating iterates is not enough: rounding can make an escaping orbit look periodic, and a slowly approaching parabolic orbit can look like an exact repetition.

Key ideas

  • The orbit of 0 classifies landmarks: it is periodic at a center, approaches a parabolic cycle at a parabolic parameter, lands on a repelling cycle at a Misiurewicz parameter, and does none of these at the Feigenbaum point.
  • Misiurewicz parameters such as −2 and i lie on the boundary; their cycle repels, so nearby parameters behave differently.
  • At a parabolic parameter, such as the cusp 1/4 or the root −3/4, a cycle has a root-of-unity multiplier, and the orbit of 0 approaches it only slowly: like 1/n at 1/4, and like 1/√n at −3/4.
  • Near a Misiurewicz parameter the Mandelbrot set looks like the Julia set near its critical value, up to a fixed scale and rotation, and both repeat themselves under magnification by the multiplier of the repelling cycle.
  • As many arms meet at a Misiurewicz point as parameter rays land there: one ray makes a tip, three or more a branch point. In the p/q limb, q spokes meet at its main branch point, whose orbit of 0 lands on α after q + 1 steps. Along an arm towards a Misiurewicz tip the decorations repeat, each time smaller by the modulus of the multiplier; a single ray alone does not make a Misiurewicz point, and a typical point of the boundary is Collet–Eckmann without being one.
  • Centers, parabolic and Misiurewicz parameters come arbitrarily close to every boundary point.

Check yourself

  1. Show that c = −i is a Misiurewicz parameter. What are its preperiod and period?

    The orbit is 0, −i, −1 − i, i, −1 − i, …, since (−1 − i)2 − i = i and i2 − i = −1 − i. It reaches the cycle {−1 − i, i} after two steps without returning to 0: preperiod 2, period 2. It is the mirror image of c = i.

  2. At c = 1/4 the error en = 1/2 − zn obeys en+1 = en − en2, starting from 1/2. Compute the first three errors. Roughly how small is en after n steps?

    e1 = 1/4, e2 = 3/16 ≈ 0.19, e3 = 39/256 ≈ 0.15. Since 1/en+1 = 1/(en(1 − en)) ≈ 1/en + 1, the reciprocal grows by about 1 per step, so en is roughly 1/n: after a thousand steps the orbit is still about 0.001 from the fixed point.

  3. At c = −3/4, find the fixed points and their multipliers. Which one is parabolic?

    The fixed points solve z2 − z − 3/4 = 0, so they are 3/2 and −1/2. The multipliers 2z are 3 and −1. The fixed point −1/2 is parabolic: its multiplier −1 is a root of unity, and after two steps it is 1. Here the period-two disk is attached to the main cardioid.

  4. The orbit of 0 for a Misiurewicz parameter c lands on the fixed point β, where only the ray at angle 0 lands. How many arms of the Mandelbrot set meet at c?

    One: c is a tip. After its start at 0 the orbit never returns to 0, the only critical point, so the map carries the rays that land at each of its later points c, z2, z3, … one-to-one onto the rays that land at the next. A single ray therefore lands at the critical value c in its Julia set, and a single parameter ray at c. The tip −2 is an example: its orbit 0, −2, 2 lands on β = 2.

  5. Is the Feigenbaum point a Misiurewicz parameter?

    No. Its critical orbit is not eventually periodic: the point is infinitely renormalizable, and its orbit of 0 never lands on a cycle.

  6. Which parameters have a critical orbit with preperiod 2 and period 1, counting from 0?

    The condition is z3 = z2, that is (c2 + c)2 + c = c2 + c, or c2(c + 1)2 = c2. So c = 0 or (c + 1)2 = 1, that is c = 0 or c = −2. At c = 0 the orbit is periodic from the start, so the only such parameter is −2, the tip.

Sources and further reading

  • Tan Lei — Similarity Between the Mandelbrot Set and Julia Sets (1990) — the original asymptotic similarity result.
  • Tomoki Kawahira — Zalcman functions and similarity between the Mandelbrot set, Julia sets, and the tricorn — a later approach to that theorem.
  • Bodil Branner and Núria Fagella — Homeomorphisms between limbs of the Mandelbrot set (Journal of Geometric Analysis, 1999) — among much else, the main branch point of each limb.
  • Jacek Graczyk and Grzegorz Świątek — Harmonic measure and expansion on the boundary of the connectedness locus (Inventiones mathematicae, 2000), and Stanislav Smirnov — Symbolic dynamics and Collet–Eckmann conditions (International Mathematics Research Notices, 2000) — what a typical point of the boundary is like.
  • Saeed Zakeri — On biaccessible points of the Mandelbrot set (Proceedings of the AMS, 2006) — a typical point of the boundary, in the sense of harmonic measure, has a single parameter ray landing on it.
  • Dzmitry Dudko and Mikhail Lyubich — MLC at Feigenbaum points (Publications mathématiques de l’IHÉS, 2026) — local connectivity at the Feigenbaum point and related parameters.
  • Jeremy Kahn, Alex Kapiamba and Mikhail Lyubich — MLC for parabolically bounded primitive renormalization (2026 preprint, not yet refereed) — recent progress for another class of parameters, not a proof of the full conjecture.
