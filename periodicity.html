Cardioids, bulbs, and periodicity

Level: IntermediateBuilds on The Mandelbrot set

The Mandelbrot set’s large smooth regions form a map of stable repeating behavior. Their shapes and attachment points encode the periods and rotations of attracting orbits.

From an attracting cycle to a visible region

The parameters c for which fc(z) = z2 + c has a finite attracting cycle form an open set inside the Mandelbrot set. Each of its connected components is a hyperbolic component. The cycle persists under small changes to c, and its exact period is constant throughout that component. The largest component has period one; the large round region to its left has period two.

A bulb is an informal name for one of these rounded components, especially a satellite component attached to another. A limb includes the associated part of the Mandelbrot set beyond an attachment point: the bulb together with its smaller components and filaments. Confusing the two leads to false statements about period, because a limb contains many different components. Usage varies: some authors, Devaney among them, use “bulb” for the whole limb.

Period is a dynamical label, not a count of visible bumps. It says how many distinct points belong to the attracting cycle. The critical orbit approaches those points, and it repeats exactly only at one parameter of the component, its center. Devaney’s discussion of bulb periods connects these attracting cycles to the main decorations.

Why the main region is a cardioid

The heart-shaped boundary is the main cardioid. Its shape follows directly from the fixed-point equation. If a is a fixed point, then a = a2 + c, so c = a − a2. The multiplier at that point is λ = 2a. Substitution gives

c(λ) = λ/2 − λ2/4.

An attracting fixed point has |⁠λ| < 1. Mapping the open unit disk of multipliers through this formula therefore gives the period-one component. Mapping the unit circle gives its boundary:

c(θ) = ½eiθ − ¼e2iθ,   0 ≤ θ < 2π.

This is the exact boundary. The center occurs at λ = 0, hence c = 0. The cusp occurs at λ = 1, hence c = 1/4. At the opposite end, λ = −1 gives c = −3/4, where the period-two component attaches. Lyubich’s book Conformal Geometry and Dynamics of Quadratic Polynomials states this parameterization as Exercise 33.2 (§33.3), with the angle measured in turns.

The word “cardioid” also appears when describing the large heart-shaped component of a miniature Mandelbrot copy. Such components resemble the main cardioid, but are generally distorted in the original parameter coordinates. The exact formula above belongs to the main period-one component of the quadratic family.

An exact calculation for the period-two disk

Points that return after two steps satisfy fc2(z) − z = 0. Factoring separates the fixed points from the two-cycle:

(z2 + c)2 + c − z = (z2 − z + c)(z2 + z + c + 1).

The roots a, b of the second factor satisfy a + b = −1 and ab = c + 1: compare z2 + z + c + 1 with (z − a)(z − b). So fc(a) = a2 + c = −1 − a = b, and in the same way fc(b) = a: the map swaps the two points. They coincide only at c = −3/4, where both equal the fixed point −1/2. This is the collision. For every other c they form a genuine two-cycle, and its multiplier is λ = (2a)(2b) = 4ab = 4(c + 1). There is no need to find a and b themselves. Attraction is therefore equivalent to

|⁠c + 1| < 1/4.

The period-two component is exactly the open disk of radius 1/4 centered at −1. Outside the disk the two-cycle still exists, but it repels. At its center, the critical point belongs to the cycle 0 ↔ −1. At its rightmost point −3/4, the cycle merges with a fixed point whose multiplier is −1. At its leftmost point −5/4, the two-cycle has multiplier −1 and the period-four satellite attaches.

Higher-period bulbs may look round at a distance, but their boundaries are not in general circles.

How the period doubles. At −3/4 the fixed point −1/2 has multiplier −1. Near it, each step moves a small error to the other side of the fixed point and keeps its size, as a negative real multiplier does. After two steps the error is back where it started. So the fixed point is almost a two-cycle, and this is where a real two-cycle splits off from it. The numbers on either side of −3/4 show the job of attracting passing from the fixed point to the two-cycle:

c Fixed point near −1/2 Two-cycle
−0.7, in the main cardioid−0.475, multiplier −0.95: attracts−0.5 ± 0.224i, multiplier 1.2: repels
−0.75, where the disk is attached−0.5, multiplier −1−0.5 twice: merged
−0.8, in the period-two disk−0.525, multiplier −1.05: repels−0.276 and −0.724, multiplier 0.8: attracts

The two cycle points come toward −1/2 from above and below the real axis, meet there, and then move apart along it.

Why exactly at −1? The points that return after two steps are the roots of fc2(z) − z. Where several of them meet, the root is a multiple root, so the derivative of fc2(z) − z vanishes there too, and (fc2)′ = 1. At a fixed point with multiplier λ, the chain rule gives (fc2)′ = λ · λ = λ2. So λ = 1 or λ = −1. At λ = 1 the two fixed points meet each other, at the cusp 1/4. At λ = −1 the two-cycle meets a fixed point.

The same happens one level up. At −5/4 the two-cycle has multiplier −1. Each return flips a small error, and after two returns, four steps, it is back. A four-cycle splits off and takes over: at c = −1.3 the two-cycle repels, with multiplier −1.2, and the four-cycle attracts. At about −1.3681 the four-cycle reaches multiplier −1 in turn, and an eight-cycle splits off, and so on: this is the period-doubling cascade. It is the case q = 2 of the satellite rule: a multiplier of −1 is half a turn, and after two returns the two halves add up to a whole turn.

Centers and multiplier coordinates

Every hyperbolic component in the quadratic Mandelbrot set has one center: the parameter where its attracting cycle is superattracting. Since the multiplier is a product of factors 2aj, it vanishes exactly when one cycle point is zero. Thus a period-n center satisfies fcn(0) = 0, with no earlier return.

For example, the period-three equation factors as

fc3(0) = c(c3 + 2c2 + c + 1) = 0.

The root zero already belongs to period one. The cubic factor gives three period-three centers: approximately −0.122561 ± 0.744862i, the rabbit and conjugate rabbit components, and −1.754878, the real period-three component. A single period can therefore occur in multiple places with different geometry and combinatorics.

Higher periods work in the same way. For period four, fc4(0) = 0 has degree 8; after removing the roots 0 and −1, which belong to periods one and two, six roots remain: the six period-four centers, among them −1.310703, the bulb on the period-two disk, and −1.940800, a minibrot near the tip −2. From degree five on no general formula gives the roots, so the centers are computed numerically, for example with Newton’s method, and their number roughly doubles with each period, as the next section shows.

The multiplier is more than a stability test: within a quadratic hyperbolic component it is a conformal coordinate, mapping that component one-to-one onto the unit disk. The center maps to zero; boundary values have modulus one. This allows internal rays to be defined by keeping the multiplier’s argument fixed while increasing its modulus. This is the Multiplier Theorem of Douady and Hubbard; Lyubich’s book proves it as Theorem 35.3.

How many components of each period?

Centers can be counted exactly. The center condition fcn(0) = 0 is a polynomial equation in c of degree 2n−1: for n = 3 it is the equation of degree 4 above. Its roots are the centers of all components whose period divides n. Each root is simple, so every center is counted exactly once, a fact known as Gleason’s lemma (Douady and Hubbard’s Orsay notes, Lemma 19.1). Writing ν(n) for the number of components of exact period n, this gives

∑d | n ν(d) = 2n−1.

The sum runs over all divisors d of n, the periods that divide n. For n = 3: ν(1) + ν(3) = 4, so ν(3) = 3, the three centers found above. For n = 4: ν(1) + ν(2) + ν(4) = 8, so ν(4) = 6. The numbers grow quickly (OEIS A000740):

The satellites are counted with the rule of the next section: a component of period d carries one satellite for each fraction p/q in lowest terms between 0 and 1, of period dq. The remaining components are primitive. Most components are primitive, and so most small copies of the Mandelbrot set are primitive too. In 2024 all centers up to period 41 were computed; for the prime period 41 there are 240 − 1 = 1,099,511,627,775 of them (Vigneron and Mihalache).

Where do all these centers lie? Give each of the 2n−1 centers of period dividing n the weight 2−(n−1). As n grows, these weights spread out over the boundary of M in the same way as the harmonic measure of M, the distribution in which a random particle coming in from far away would hit the set (Genadi Levin, 1990; restated by Dujardin and Favre, Corollary 2). So the centers of high period crowd near the parts of the boundary that are easy to reach from outside. The real axis is not one of them: its harmonic measure is zero (Zakeri), and indeed the share of real components falls from 1/3 in period 3 to 170 of 2,010, about 8%, in period 12.

Why each center counts once. Write Gn(c) = fcn(0), so that G1 = c and Gn = Gn−12 + c. The proof uses one idea from algebra. A number is an algebraic integer if it is a root of a polynomial with integer coefficients and leading coefficient 1 (a monic polynomial). The period-three centers found above, such as −1.754878, are examples: they are roots of c3 + 2c2 + c + 1. Algebraic integers behave like ordinary integers in two ways that matter here:

  • sums and products of algebraic integers are again algebraic integers;
  • the only fractions among them are the whole numbers. If a fraction p/q in lowest terms is a root of such a polynomial of degree k, multiplying the equation by qk−1 shows that pk/q is a whole number, so q = 1.

Gn has integer coefficients and leading coefficient 1, so every root c is an algebraic integer. So are Gn−1(c) and Gn−1′(c), which are built from c by sums and products. Differentiating gives Gn′ = 2Gn−1Gn−1′ + 1. If Gn′(c) were 0, the product Gn−1(c)·Gn−1′(c) would be −1/2: an algebraic integer that is a fraction but not a whole number, which is impossible. So the derivative is not 0 at any root, and no root is repeated, since a root is repeated exactly when the derivative is 0 there too.

Rational angles and the periods of satellite bulbs

Follow a period-n component to a boundary parameter where its cycle multiplier is λ = e2πi p/q, with p/q reduced and q ≥ 2. A satellite component of period nq attaches there. On the boundary the old cycle neither attracts nor repels: near each of its points, one return, n steps of the map, acts like a turn by p/q of a full turn. After q returns these turns add up to whole turns, so the multiplier of q returns is λq = 1, as at the collision that creates the period-two disk. Crossing into the satellite, the old cycle starts to repel, and q new points split off around each of its points: an attracting cycle of nq points. On the period-two disk, where λ = 4(c + 1), λ = −1 at c = −5/4 gives the period-four bulb, and λ = e2πi/3 at c = −9/8 + (√3/8)i ≈ −1.125 + 0.217i gives a bulb of period six.

For satellites attached directly to the main cardioid, n = 1, so the denominator q is their period. The fraction p/q is the rotation number, and it can be seen in the Julia set. For every c in the p/q bulb, the repelling fixed point called α is a meeting point of q arms of the Julia set, and the map turns these arms like a wheel, advancing each by p places out of q. In Douady’s rabbit, from the 1/3 bulb, the three main lobes, the body and the two ears, meet at α ≈ −0.276 + 0.480i, and each step of the map carries one lobe to the next. The external rays at angles 1/7, 2/7 and 4/7 land at α, and doubling permutes them, 1/7 → 2/7 → 4/7 → 1/7: a rotation by one third (Milnor). The same q can be counted in the parameter plane: q spokes of the limb’s antenna meet at its main branch point.

The coordinates follow by substituting e2πi p/q into the cardioid equation. Notice that the internal angle 1/3 is not the ordinary polar angle of its attachment measured from c = 0. Internal coordinates belong to multiplier space.

These fractions have a Farey-tree organization. For neighboring fractions p/q and r/s with |⁠ps − rq| = 1, their mediant is (p + r)/(q + s). Between 1/3 and 1/2, the next lowest-denominator label is 2/5; between 1/3 and 2/5 it is 3/8. Repeating this between neighbors gives the fractions 1/2, 1/3, 2/5, 3/8, 5/13, …, with the Fibonacci numbers as denominators (Devaney, 1999; Devaney’s exploration of bulb rotation numbers). The bulbs with periods 2, 3, 5, 8, 13, … therefore approach, alternating from side to side, the boundary point with internal angle (3 − √5)/2 ≈ 0.382. It is the mirror image of the point with internal angle (√5 − 1)/2, where the map has the classical golden-mean Siegel disk.

Roots, primitive components, and satellites

The root of a hyperbolic component is its distinguished boundary point where the component’s multiplier coordinate tends to one. For a satellite, this is its point of attachment to its parent. At that shared parameter, the actual parabolic orbit has the parent’s smaller period: the child’s multiplier tends to one because it records a longer return.

A primitive component does not meet a lower-period parent at its root. Its root has a parabolic orbit of the same exact period as the component. Primitive components supply the heart-shaped cores of primitive miniature Mandelbrot copies. “Primitive component” and “primitive period” are separate uses of the word: primitive period simply means least period for any cycle.

Roots other than the main cardioid cusp are landing points of two periodic external parameter rays. The cusp at c = 1/4 has the single angle-zero ray. The pair of rays describes a wake, a region on the far side of the root that contains the corresponding limb. This is a precise combinatorial replacement for judging attachments by eye. See Milnor’s account of periodic orbits, external rays, and the Mandelbrot set.

Period doubling and its limit

The real-axis chain starts with a stable fixed point, loses stability at c = −3/4, and continues with a stable two-cycle. At −5/4 a four-cycle appears; the box How the period doubles explains both steps. Further bifurcations produce periods 8, 16, 32, … . The parameter intervals become smaller and accumulate at the real Feigenbaum parameter, approximately −1.401155189.

Period doubling is the satellite rule with q = 2 repeated indefinitely. Its limit, the Feigenbaum point, is an infinitely renormalizable parameter: it lies in infinitely many minibrots, each inside the one before, those whose main components have period 2, 4, 8, …, and it has no attracting cycle at all. The lengths of the doubling intervals shrink by a ratio approaching Feigenbaum’s δ ≈ 4.669, the universal number described in the minibrots chapter and, for experiments, in the chapter on chaos.

Period-doubling cascades also occur inside other periodic structures. A period-three component can have a period-six child, then period twelve, and so on. Other rational satellite angles produce other period multiplications, so the periods of the Mandelbrot set are far richer than the single sequence 1, 2, 4, 8, … .

Neutral boundaries and slow dynamics

At a root-of-unity boundary multiplier, the cycle is parabolic. Attraction can persist through particular regions called petals, but is slower than the geometric convergence of a strictly attracting cycle. Nearby escaping orbits may spend a very long time near this almost-neutral cycle. Thin bright bands and elaborate patterns near cusps often reflect these large escape times.

At irrational internal angles, the boundary cycle is irrationally neutral, and the map has a Siegel disk or a Cremer point. The rational angles, where satellites attach, are dense on the boundary of each component, but the irrational ones fill most of it. The chapter on neutral cycles describes these cases.

All of this concerns components with an attracting cycle. Whether every component of the interior of the Mandelbrot set is of this kind is the open problem of density of hyperbolicity; the chapter on open questions explains what is known about it.

Key ideas

  • A hyperbolic component is a connected region of parameters with an attracting cycle of a fixed period; its center is the one parameter where the critical point lies on that cycle.
  • The main cardioid is c = λ/2 − λ2/4 for |⁠λ| < 1, and the period-two component is the disk |⁠c + 1| < 1/4; both follow from a few lines of algebra.
  • The multiplier is a coordinate on each component, and at the boundary point where it equals e2πip/q a satellite with q times the period is attached.
  • There are 2n−1 centers of period dividing n, each counted once; most components are primitive.
  • The rotation number p/q of a bulb can be seen in its Julia sets, whose q arms at the fixed point α are turned by p places at each step.

Check yourself

  1. Find the point where the bulb with internal angle 1/2 is attached, and the period of that bulb.

    With λ = eπi = −1, c = −1/2 − 1/4 = −3/4. The denominator is 2, so the bulb has period 2: it is the period-two disk |⁠c + 1| < 1/4, which indeed touches −3/4.

  2. For c = −1.2, what is the multiplier of the two-cycle? Is it attracting?

    λ = 4(c + 1) = 4 · (−0.2) = −0.8. Its modulus is less than 1, so the two-cycle attracts; −1.2 lies in the period-two disk, since |⁠−1.2 + 1| = 0.2 < 1/4.

  3. How many components of exact period 5 are there? Use the counting formula.

    The divisors of 5 are 1 and 5, so ν(1) + ν(5) = 24 = 16 and ν(5) = 15. Of these, 4 are satellites of the main cardioid (internal angles 1/5, 2/5, 3/5, 4/5) and 11 are primitive.

  4. Which period does the bulb between the 1/3 and 2/5 bulbs on the main cardioid have, the one with the smallest denominator?

    The mediant of 1/3 and 2/5 is (1 + 2)/(3 + 5) = 3/8, so the bulb has period 8.

  5. Why can the center of a hyperbolic component not lie on the boundary of the Mandelbrot set?

    At the center the cycle is superattracting, with multiplier 0. Small changes of c change the multiplier only slightly, so it stays smaller than 1 in modulus, and the cycle stays attracting: all nearby parameters belong to the same component, so the center is an interior point.
