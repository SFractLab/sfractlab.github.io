Area, dimension and famous numbers

Level: IntermediateBuilds on The Mandelbrot set and External rays & angles

How large is the Mandelbrot set, how rough is its edge, and which numbers are hidden in it? Some answers are exact, some are bounds proved by hand or by computer, and some are estimates that no proof yet supports. This chapter collects them and says which is which.

Areas you can compute exactly

Two pieces of M have exact areas. The main cardioid is the image of the unit disk under the multiplier map c = λ/2 − λ2/4 (why the main region is a cardioid). This map is one-to-one on the disk, and it stretches areas by |dc/dλ|⁠2 = |1 − λ|⁠2/4, so the cardioid’s area is

∫∫|λ|<1 |1 − λ|2/4 dA = (π + π/2)/4 = 3π/8 ≈ 1.1781.

The integral of |1 − λ|⁠2 = 1 − 2 Re λ + |λ|⁠2 over the unit disk is π + π/2, because the middle term averages to zero. The period-two disk has radius 1/4 and area π/16 ≈ 0.1963 (the period-two disk). The two touch only at −3/4, so together they prove that the area of M is at least 7π/16 ≈ 1.3744. Every other component adds a little more, but no other component’s area is known in closed form; the certified map of the computing chapter proves 1.377 by checking pixels instead.

Counting pixels

The most precise number comes from counting. Thorsten Förstemann classified about 88 trillion pixels on graphics cards in 2012, in 35 days of computing, and estimated the area as 1.5065918849 ± 0.0000000028 (Förstemann’s report). The often-quoted 1.50659177 ± 0.00000008 is an earlier count, Robert Munafo’s of 1999 (Munafo’s history of area estimates). The ± is a statistical confidence interval, from twenty grids with randomly varied spacing and offset. It is not a bound: a pixel that has not escaped within the budget may still escape, thin parts of M fall between the pixels (a picture is a computation), and no count can tell whether the boundary of M, which no pixel resolves, has positive area.

An upper bound from the outside

A rigorous upper bound comes from the coordinate on the outside of M (the rays chapter). Its inverse Ψ maps the outside of the unit disk one-to-one onto the outside of M, and for large |w| it has a Laurent series

Ψ(w) = w + b0 + b1/w + b2/w2 + ⋯ = w − 1/2 + 1/(8w) − 1/(4w2) + 15/(128w3) + 0/w4 − ⋯

The leading coefficient is exactly 1: in the language of potential theory, M has logarithmic capacity 1. The coefficients are rational numbers whose denominators are powers of 2, and they can be computed one after another from the condition that fcn(c), the critical value after n more steps, written in terms of w, equals w2n up to terms that tend to zero (Irwin Jungreis, 1985; John Ewing and Glenn Schober, 1992). Gronwall’s area theorem of 1914 then gives the area exactly:

area(M) = π (1 − Σm≥1 m |bm|2).

Every term of the sum is positive or zero, so stopping after N terms leaves an upper bound AN. The bounds fall very slowly. With 10, 100 and 2,000 terms they are about 2.407, 2.053 and 1.856. Ewing and Schober reached 1.7274 with 240,000 terms; Chen, Kawahira, Li and Yuan reached 1.703927 with a million (2011); and Daniel Bittner, Long Cheong, Dante Gates and Hieu Nguyen reached 1.68288 with five million, computing in floating point with an estimated error of at most 3 × 10−9 (Bittner, Cheong, Gates and Nguyen, 2017).

The coefficients themselves are only partly understood. Many of them vanish: Levin, and independently Ewing and Schober, proved that bm = 0 whenever m = (2k + 1)2ν with k + 3 ≤ 2ν, which covers b4, b8, b12 and infinitely many more. They do not decrease faster than 1/m by any fixed power: there are no constants K and ε > 0 with |bm| < K/m1+ε for every m (Bielefeld, Fisher and von Haeseler, 1993). If Σ |bm| were finite, Ψ would extend continuously to the unit circle, and M would be locally connected, which is the open MLC conjecture.

Squeezing the area between squares

A second route covers the plane with small squares and decides each one with the distance estimate. Koebe’s quarter theorem turns the estimate at a square’s center into a proved lower bound for the distance to M, so a square whose half-diagonal is smaller than that bound misses M; a similar estimate from inside an attracting cycle shows that a square lies in the interior. The squares that remain undecided are cut into four and examined again. The squares proved inside give a lower bound for the area, and adding the undecided ones gives an upper bound. Yuval Fisher and Jay Hill bounded the area this way between about 1.503 and 1.570, and Thorsten Förstemann, with squares down to 2−26 of the starting square’s side and months of computing, showed in 2017 that it lies between 1.50640 and 1.53121 (Förstemann’s 2017 report). These computations ran in ordinary floating point and have not been formally verified. Geoffrey Irving wrote the upper-bound half of this method in the proof assistant Lean, with a proof that the program and its interval arithmetic are correct; running it gives the weaker bound 1.826454 without trusting any floating-point computation (Irving’s ray-render). The certified map of the computing chapter is a coarse relative of these computations.

So the gap between the Laurent bound 1.68 and the estimate 1.5066 is mostly slow convergence: the series would need astronomically many more terms. The square bounds also limit the boundary. The squares proved inside lie in the interior of M, so the boundary can have area at most 1.53121 − 1.50640 ≈ 0.025. Whether that area is zero remains open.

How rough is the boundary?

Hausdorff dimension measures how a set fills space at small scales: a smooth curve has dimension 1, a region of the plane dimension 2, and the dimension can be any number in between (fractals). Mitsuhiro Shishikura proved, in a paper published in 1998, that the boundary of the Mandelbrot set has dimension 2, the largest possible in the plane, and indeed that every piece of it does: its intersection with any open set that meets it has dimension 2 (Shishikura, Theorem A). The engine of the proof is parabolic implosion. The same paper shows that the Julia sets of the parameters in a dense, generic part of the boundary also have dimension 2 (Theorem B).

Dimension 2 does not imply positive area, and a set without interior can still have positive area. Whether the boundary of M has positive area is open as of October 2026; it is conjectured to be zero. The amount of ink in a picture settles neither question: it depends on the resolution, the method and the iteration budget, and the Julia set at c = −2, a whole segment, can vanish between two rows of pixels.

The dimension of a Julia set

For each parameter, the Julia set Jc has its own dimension d(c), and as a function of c it shows where the dynamics changes. At c = 0 the Julia set is a circle and at c = −2 a segment, so d = 1 there. For every other parameter in M, d(c) > 1 (Anna Zdunik, 1990). Near 0, d(c) = 1 + |c|2/(4 log 2) + ⋯ (David Ruelle, 1982). Far outside M the Julia set is a thin Cantor dust, and d(c) behaves like 2 log 2 / log(4|c|), which tends to 0. Curtis McMullen computed many values: about 1.268 for the basilica, 1.393 for Douady’s rabbit and 1.081 at the cusp c = 1/4 (McMullen, Hausdorff dimension and conformal dynamics III).

The function is smooth where the dynamics is stable: it depends real-analytically on c in every hyperbolic component and outside M (Ruelle). At the boundary it can jump. Along the real axis it is continuous as c increases to 1/4, but not as c decreases to 1/4 from outside M: just beyond the cusp, the imploded Julia sets keep a dimension larger than d(1/4) (continuity from the left: Bodart and Michel Zinsmeister, 1996; the jump: Adrien Douady, Pierrette Sentenac and Zinsmeister, 1997). This is the bifurcation locus of where Julia sets change, seen through a number.

A different measurement gives a different answer. The harmonic measure of a connected Julia set, the distribution in which a random particle coming in from far away hits it, always has dimension exactly 1, however rough the set (Nikolai Makarov, 1985). It is concentrated on a small part of the Julia set, which is why random inverse iteration, which follows this measure, misses the deep fjords (Julia sets backwards).

Julia sets with positive area

A Julia set has no interior, but it can still have positive area. For most parameters it does not: Mikhail Lyubich proved in 1991 that the Julia set of z2 + c has zero area whenever the map has no irrationally neutral cycle and is not infinitely renormalizable (Lyubich). The exceptions are real. Xavier Buff and Arnaud Chéritat constructed quadratic Julia sets of positive area, with Siegel disks, with Cremer points, or with infinitely many satellite renormalizations (Buff and Chéritat, 2012). Artur Avila and Mikhail Lyubich found Feigenbaum Julia sets of positive area (Avila and Lyubich, 2022), while the Julia set of the classical real Feigenbaum polynomial has dimension less than 2 and therefore zero area (Artem Dudko and Scott Sutherland, 2020).

π in the Mandelbrot set

In 1991 Dave Boll tried to show that the neck of M at −3/4, where the main cardioid meets the period-two disk, has zero width. He counted the steps N that the orbit of 0 needs to leave the disk of radius 2 for c = −3/4 + εi, and found π (Gerald Edgar’s page, which links Boll’s post). Recomputed in double precision:

ε0.10.010.00110−410−510−610−7
N333153,14331,417314,1603,141,59331,415,927

So Nε approaches π. The same happens at the cusp, where N√ε approaches π for c = 1/4 + ε, and the chapter on neutral cycles shows where the π comes from: the orbit creeps through a narrow gate, and the passage time is an integral that gives an arctangent. At −5/4, the root of the period-four bulb on the period-two disk, the scale is π/2, and the path matters even more. Along the parabola c = −5/4 − t2 + ti, the path of the original experiments, the product Nt is close to π/2 for most small t, including every t = 10−k; but for a few percent of the values of t, at every scale we tested, it lands near a larger whole multiple of π/2 instead. Straight up the line −5/4 + ti, it lands near such multiples for most t.

Aaron Klebanoff proved the cusp case in 2001 (Klebanoff), and Paul Siewert gave a sharper, conceptual proof in 2023. In 2025 Thies Brockmoeller, Oscar Scherz and Nedim Srkalovic proved the same limits at −3/4 and −5/4 for parameters that approach along the parameter rays landing there, or along paths close to them, and a version for every parabolic parameter of M: approaching the root of a satellite component along one of its two parameter rays, N·|c − c0| tends to π divided by a constant that the multiplier map determines, and at a primitive root |c − c0| is replaced by its square root (Brockmoeller, Scherz and Srkalovic, a preprint). The experiments’ own paths are not quite covered: it is not even known whether the vertical line through −3/4 meets M anywhere except at −3/4 itself.

The experiment is also a lesson in floating-point arithmetic. At the cusp, double precision gives 31,414 for ε = 10−8 and 314,157 for 10−10, but 3,141,625 for 10−12, where the error has stopped shrinking, and 31,430,913 for 10−14, which gives 3.1431 instead of 3.1416. At the cusp the main culprit is the parameter itself: 1/4 + 10−12 cannot be stored exactly, the nearest double is about 1/4 + 0.99998 × 10−12, and iterating that parameter with 34 digits gives 3,141,625 again, while the exact parameter gives 3,141,591. At the neck, ε = 10−8 gives about 7.85 billion steps in double precision, 25π instead of π, while a computation with 34 digits gives the expected 314,159,266.

Feigenbaum’s constants

Along the real axis the period-doubling cascade, with periods 1, 2, 4, 8, …, accumulates at the Feigenbaum point, also called the Myrberg point after Pekka Myrberg, who studied the real family in the 1950s and 1960s:

cF = −1.40115518909205060052…

The gaps between successive period doublings shrink by ratios that approach Feigenbaum’s δ = 4.66920160910299067185…, and the cycles shrink in space, near the critical point, by factors approaching α = 2.50290787509589282228… (Feigenbaum, 1978; the digits were computed by Keith Briggs in 1991). The same two numbers appear for every family of maps with a single quadratic maximum. The minibrots chapter shows δ emerging from the centers of the cascade, and the external angle of the Feigenbaum point, 0.41245403364…, whose binary digits are the Thue–Morse sequence, is derived in the section on tuning.

Exact numbers in the landscape

Many landmarks of M are algebraic numbers, roots of polynomials with integer coefficients, and some have pleasant forms.

  • The tip and the cusp. The real slice of M is exactly the interval [−2, 1/4]: the tip −2 is a Misiurewicz point, the cusp 1/4 a parabolic one.
  • The plastic constant. The centers of period 3 solve c(c + 1)2 = −1. With u = c2 + c this becomes c = −u2 with u3 = u + 1. The real root of u3 = u + 1 is the plastic constant ρ ≈ 1.324718, so the center of the airplane is −ρ2 ≈ −1.754878, and the two complex roots give Douady’s rabbit and its mirror image, −0.122561 ± 0.744862i. The period-three window that the airplane opens on the real axis starts exactly at −7/4 (the guided tour).
  • The main antenna. The point −1.5436890127 on the real antenna, where the parameter rays 5/12 and 7/12 land and two chaotic bands of the bifurcation diagram merge, is the real root of c3 + 2c2 + 2c + 2 = 0: there the orbit 0, c, c2 + c reaches the repelling fixed point α at its third step and stays there.
  • How many components. There are 2n−1 centers of period dividing n (counting components). In 2024 François Vigneron and Nicolae Mihalache computed all of them up to period 41; since 41 is prime, there are 240 − 1 = 1,099,511,627,775 components of period exactly 41 (How to split a tera-polynomial).

Key ideas

  • The main cardioid has area 3π/8 and the period-two disk π/16, so the area of M is at least 7π/16 ≈ 1.3744.
  • Pixel counts estimate the area as about 1.50659; the estimate is precise but not a proof.
  • Gronwall’s area theorem turns the Laurent coefficients of the map onto the outside of M into upper bounds, which fall very slowly: the best published is about 1.68288. Covering the plane with squares decided by distance estimates gives, by computer, 1.50640 ≤ area ≤ 1.53121.
  • The boundary of M has Hausdorff dimension 2 (Shishikura); whether it has positive area is open.
  • The dimension of the Julia set is 1 at c = 0 and −2, larger than 1 elsewhere in M, smooth on hyperbolic components, and discontinuous at the cusp; the harmonic measure of a connected Julia set always has dimension 1.
  • Escape counts near −3/4, 1/4 and −5/4 approach π (or π/2) after rescaling, now proved, along parameter rays, at every parabolic parameter; Feigenbaum’s δ and α and the plastic constant are other numbers built into the set.

Check yourself

  1. Why does a partial sum AN = π(1 − Σm≤N m|bm|2) give an upper bound, not an estimate that could be too low?

    The full area is π(1 − Σ m|bm|2) over all m. Every term m|bm|2 is positive or zero, so leaving out the terms after N subtracts less than the full sum, and AN is at least the area.

  2. Check that the period-two disk and the main cardioid together have area 7π/16, and that this is about 91% of the pixel-count estimate.

    3π/8 + π/16 = 6π/16 + π/16 = 7π/16 ≈ 1.3744, and 1.3744/1.5066 ≈ 0.912.

  3. Using N√ε ≈ π, about how many steps does the orbit of 0 need to escape for c = 1/4 + 10−10?

    About π/√(10−10) = π · 105 ≈ 314,159. Double precision gives 314,157.

  4. Show that the centers of period dividing 3 other than 0 satisfy c(c + 1)2 = −1, and that u = c2 + c then satisfies u3 = u + 1.

    The orbit of 0 is 0, c, c2 + c, (c2 + c)2 + c = c(c(c + 1)2 + 1). For c ≠ 0 the third point is 0 exactly when c(c + 1)2 = −1. Then u2 = c2(c + 1)2 = c · c(c + 1)2 = −c, so c = −u2, and u = c2 + c = u4 − u2. Dividing by u ≠ 0 gives u3 = u + 1.

  5. Why does Shishikura’s theorem, that the boundary of M has dimension 2, not settle whether it has positive area?

    Positive area implies dimension 2, but not conversely: there are sets of dimension 2 and area zero, for example sets built like the Cantor set whose pieces shrink only slowly. So dimension 2 is consistent with both answers.

  6. The Julia set of z2 − 2 is the segment [−2, 2], of dimension 1. Why can its dimension not be smaller, although it has no area?

    A segment contains a curve, and any set containing a curve has dimension at least 1. Dimension below 1 occurs only for disconnected Julia sets, the Cantor dust of parameters outside M, where it tends to 0 as |c| grows.
