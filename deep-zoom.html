How deep zooms are computed

Level: IntermediateBuilds on Computing the pictures and Minibrots & self-similarity

Deep-zoom pictures show the Mandelbrot set magnified 10100, 101000 times or more. Ordinary computer numbers give out at about 1013, and computing every pixel with enough digits would take days or years per picture. Modern programs need minutes. This chapter explains how: one orbit computed with many digits, every pixel as a small difference from it, and shortcuts that skip millions of iterations at a time.

The pipeline at a glance

Take a real example. In 2021 a user called Fractal universe posted a location near c ≈ −1.7499 on the fractalforums.org forum, at a magnification of 6.5·10137. The view is filled by a minibrot whose nucleus has period 43,292,334, and its pixels need between 292 million and 2.1 billion iterations before they escape (Heiland-Allen’s counts). Computed directly, with about 500 binary digits per number, even the smallest of these counts for each of 2048 × 1024 pixels would keep one processor core busy for more than four years. Zhuoran, the author of the program Imagina, reported rendering it at that size in 150 seconds.

Every modern deep-zoom renderer does this in five stages.

  1. A reference orbit. One orbit, usually that of the image’s center, is computed with as many digits as the depth requires.
  2. Perturbation. Every pixel follows the reference as a small difference from it, computed with ordinary hardware numbers whose exponent range is extended in software.
  3. Rebasing. When a pixel’s orbit comes closer to 0 than to the reference, the pixel restarts the reference from its beginning, which keeps the small differences accurate.
  4. Skipping. Precomputed linear maps let each pixel jump over thousands or millions of iterations at once.
  5. Coloring. The escape count, smoothed, and the distance estimate are computed as in the chapter on computing the pictures.

The rest of the chapter takes these stages one at a time, after two questions that come first: how many digits a deep zoom needs, and why digits are expensive.

How many digits a deep zoom needs

A picture W pixels wide of a window of width w has pixel spacing s = w/W. To give every pixel its own parameter, the coordinates must tell numbers s apart near |c| ≈ 1, which takes about log2(1/s) binary digits (bits). For a picture 1,920 pixels wide at magnification 10k that is about 3.32k + 12 bits: 345 bits (104 decimal digits) at 10100, 3,330 bits at 101000, 33,000 bits at 1010000. Programs add a safety margin: 20 decimal digits in Kalles Fraktaler, 24 bits in Fraktaler 3, 64 in Imagina and 120 in FractalShark (their source code, read for this atlas). None of them multiplies the digits by the number of iterations.

That is surprising, because the iteration is chaotic. Near the boundary each step roughly doubles small errors, as in the chaos chapter, so after a few hundred steps the computed orbit has nothing in common with the true orbit of the pixel’s center. In experiments for this atlas, with orbits of 7,000 and 60,000 steps computed with a few more bits than log2(1/s), the error in zn grew to the size of zn itself. Yet in every test, with orbits of 270 to 60,000 steps, the computed orbit was, to high accuracy, the exact orbit of a parameter only about 2−m pixels away, where m is the number of extra bits, however long the orbit.

The reason is that the derivative dzn/dc grows just as fast as the errors do. A rounding error made at step k changes the orbit from then on in the same way as a change of c by the error divided by dzk/dc, which is tiny. The computed picture is therefore an exact picture of a grid of pixels shifted by a small fraction of a pixel, which is as good as the exact picture: antialiasing moves samples by more than that on purpose. This way of judging a computation, by the change of input that would explain its result, is called backward error, and it will return at the end of the chapter.

The escape counts of individual pixels are another matter. Near a deep minibrot they are extremely sensitive: in one test window, moving every pixel by 2−24 of a pixel width still changed 5% of the counts. Comparing counts with a more precise computation therefore says little about whether a picture is right; the backward error does.

More digits or more range

A floating-point number is a mantissa, its significant digits, times a power of two, its exponent. The two parts buy different things. Mantissa bits decide how finely numbers of similar size can be told apart; exponent bits decide how small or large a number can be at all.

Computing every pixel directly needs mantissa: c = −0.74… must be stored to within a fraction of the pixel spacing. Perturbation, below, needs the opposite. A pixel’s difference from the reference is tiny, but it only has to be known relative to itself, to a few bits more than it takes to number the pixels across the picture: Fraktaler 3 asks for a mantissa longer than log2 of the image diagonal in pixels, about 11 bits. What it does need is exponent range, down to 10−1000 at a magnification of 101000, far below the 10−308 where doubles round to 0. Kalles Fraktaler therefore uses doubles up to about 10290 and, for z2 + c, doubles rescaled in software at any greater depth; for formulas that cannot be rescaled it moves to long doubles and, beyond about 104900, to floatexp (its manual and source code). Graphics processors calculate fastest in single precision, so renderers that use them combine floats with an extra exponent: FractalShark pairs two 32-bit floats with a shared exponent for an effective 48-bit mantissa.

Why many digits are slow

A processor multiplies two doubles in one instruction, several per clock cycle. A number with P bits is stored as P/64 machine words, and the school method of multiplication multiplies every word of one number by every word of the other: (P/64)2 products, so doubling the digits quadruples the work. Faster methods take over as numbers grow. Karatsuba’s trick needs three half-size products instead of four, so its cost grows like P1.585; the Toom–Cook methods split numbers into more parts; methods based on the fast Fourier transform are nearly linear. The GMP library, on which most deep-zoom programs build, switches to Karatsuba from about 1,200 bits (magnifications of about 10360), to Toom–Cook from about 8,000 bits (102300) and to the FFT only from about 200,000 to 300,000 bits (1060,000 and beyond), on an AMD Zen 2 processor (GMP manual; GMP’s tuning tables). Even at 53 bits, arbitrary precision is 50 to 100 times slower than hardware doubles, because of the bookkeeping around every operation.

One step of z2 + c takes three real multiplications. Measured for this atlas with the MPFR library, one step costs 0.24 µs at 400 bits (magnification 10100), 2.2 µs at 3,400 bits (101000) and 76 µs at 34,000 bits (1010000), against 1.2 ns in doubles. With perturbation, the work for the pixels no longer depends on the depth at all. Only the reference orbit becomes more expensive, and it is computed once per picture.

Perturbation

Let the reference have parameter C and orbit Z0 = 0, Zn+1 = Zn2 + C, computed once with all the digits and then stored rounded to an ordinary number format. A pixel with parameter c = C + Δc has orbit zn = Zn + δn with δ0 = 0. Subtracting the two iterations gives, exactly,

δn+1 = 2Znδn + δn2 + Δc.

This is algebra, not an approximation. The dangerous operation of direct iteration, adding a tiny number to a number of size 1, has been done on paper: the large terms Zn2 + C cancel exactly. What is left only multiplies small numbers by numbers of size 1, and multiplication keeps relative precision. So δn can be computed in hardware numbers, and its digits stay meaningful, as long as the exponent can reach its size. The pixel’s full value Zn + δn is formed only to test for escape. For the distance estimate, the pixel’s derivative Dn = dzn/dc needs no special treatment: Dn+1 = 2(Zn + δn)Dn + 1 is computed in the same ordinary numbers.

K. I. Martin published this method in 2013 with his program SuperFractalThing, in a two-page note that starts the reference at c rather than at 0, which shifts the same recurrence by one step (Martin, 2013). The effect was dramatic. A year later the user Pauldelbrot described rendering a picture at a magnification of about 10157 with “just three dozen points iterated at 163 decimals of precision, instead of the one-and-one-quarter-million at that precision to render this same image in conventional software” (fractalforums.com, April 2014). Why three dozen points and not one is the subject of the next section.

Glitches

Perturbation has one weak spot. Suppose a pixel’s orbit passes close to 0 while the reference does not. Then δn ≈ −Zn, and the pixel’s value zn = Zn + δn is the small difference of two nearly equal numbers. δn carries 53 bits relative to its own size, which is about |Zn|, so zn is known only to about 2−53|Zn|: at |zn| = 10−3|Zn| ten bits are lost, at 10−16|Zn| all of them. Pauldelbrot analyzed this in April 2014: the difference between two neighboring pixels is multiplied at each step by about twice the pixel’s own value, so when that value is small, the differences between neighbors fall below the resolution of δ. Neighboring pixels then round to the same δ and follow the same orbit from then on.

These failures are called glitches. Where neighbors snap together completely, a whole region gets the same count: a flat blob of one color where there should be structure. Where they lose only part of their difference, the counts are scrambled into noise or wrong but plausible-looking patterns. Glitches appear near minibrots and embedded Julia sets other than the one the reference belongs to, because pixels there return close to 0 at multiples of a different period. A second failure is simpler: the reference may escape before the pixels do, and then pixels that need more iterations have nothing left to follow.

Pauldelbrot’s test flags a pixel when |zn| < 10−3|Zn|; Kalles Fraktaler uses the squared form |zn|2 < 10−7|Zn|2. Flagged pixels were then computed again with a second reference placed in the middle of the glitched region, and so on until no glitches were left: Kalles Fraktaler rendered a test picture at 1280 × 720 pixels with seven extra references in under 1.5 minutes in 2014, and dozens of references were common for difficult pictures. No threshold was ever shown to catch all glitches without false alarms. In a test window at width 3.6·10−31, about 1% of the pixels were glitched; the test flagged 2.4% of all pixels, more than half of them correctly computed ones, and missed some of the wrong ones.

Where the reference comes from

Before 2021 the choice of reference mattered for correctness: it had to stay bounded at least as long as every pixel, and the nucleus of a minibrot in view was the natural choice. Today all four programs whose source code was read for this atlas, Kalles Fraktaler, Fraktaler 3, Imagina and FractalShark, simply use the image center: rebasing, in the next section, made any reference work. Finding the nucleus of a minibrot is still important, for navigation (“zoom to the next minibrot”) and for speed, and it is a nice piece of mathematics.

Guessing the period. Instead of one point, iterate a whole small disk of parameters around the view’s center. As long as its image under c ↦ zn(c) is small, it is approximately a disk of radius r·|dzn/dc| around zn, where r is the radius of the view. The first n at which this image disk covers 0,

|zn| < r·|dzn/dc|,

signals that, to first order, some parameter in the view has zn(c) = 0: a nucleus of period n, belonging to the largest minibrot in or near the view. This ball method needs one orbit and its derivative; Robert Munafo’s older box method iterates the four corners of a square instead. A related signal comes from a single point: the steps at which |zn| becomes smaller than ever before are the periods of the minibrots whose “atom domains” contain the point (Heiland-Allen, 2021).

Newton’s method. Once the period p is known, the nucleus solves zp(c) = 0, and Newton’s method finds it as in the computing chapter: c ← c − zp/z′p, with z′n+1 = 2znz′n + 1. Each step iterates the orbit and its derivative p times with all the digits. Near the root the number of correct digits doubles with each step, so few steps are needed. At Fractal universe’s location, starting from the published center, two steps of 52 and 38 seconds, each of 43.3 million iterations at 600 bits, were enough. Imagina and FractalShark save time by computing the derivative with fewer digits, about a quarter of the depth in bits; with too few, the convergence becomes linear. Newton’s method can also wander off: even starting points close to one nucleus can lead to another, so programs check that the result stayed inside the view.

The size of the minibrot. Near a nucleus of period p, p steps of the iteration act like one step of Z ↦ Z2 + C in rescaled coordinates, the renormalization of the minibrots chapter. The scale factor follows from the nucleus orbit: with λk = 2z1 · 2z2 ⋯ 2zk, λ = λp−1 and β = 1 + 1/λ1 + ⋯ + 1/λp−1, the minibrot is a copy of M scaled by about 1/|βλ2| and turned by the angle −arg(βλ2) (Heiland-Allen, 2016, after Hunt and Ott). For the period-43,292,334 minibrot the factor is 10−138.2, matching the magnification of the published view.

Periodic references. The orbit of a nucleus returns exactly to 0 after p steps and then repeats, so it never escapes, and p stored values describe all of it. At Fractal universe’s location the pixels need up to 2.1 billion iterations, but the reference has only 43.3 million values: 0.69 gigabytes instead of 34. Imagina and FractalShark go one step further and do not need the nucleus at all: they stop the orbit of the image center at the first step where the ball test fires, and treat what they have as one period. Pixels that run past its end simply restart it, which is the end-of-reference case of rebasing. Misiurewicz points, whose orbits are eventually periodic too, have been suggested as references, but none of the four programs uses them.

Rebasing: one reference is enough

On 3 August 2021 the user Zhuoran posted a few lines of pseudocode on fractalforums.org under the title “Another solution to perturbation glitches”. A pixel keeps two counters, its own iteration n and its position m on the reference. After each step it forms its value z = Zm + δ, and

if |Zm + δ| < |δ|, or the reference has ended: δ ← Zm + δ and m ← 0.

The pixel’s own count n carries on. This is rebasing. It is exact, because the perturbation formula only needs Z to be some orbit of z2 + C and δ to be the pixel’s value minus the current reference value; nothing ties m to n. Restarting at m = 0, where Z0 = 0, makes the new δ equal to the pixel’s value itself, and Δc is unchanged. And it avoids glitches instead of detecting them: the switch happens when the pixel is about as close to 0 as δ is large, before any serious cancellation, and afterwards δ is the small number z with its full relative precision. Having passed near 0, the pixel follows an orbit that resembles the beginning of the reference, which is where it now compares itself.

Zhuoran found it “very reliable, even when single precision is used”. Claude Heiland-Allen tested it in November 2021, finding that one reference gave a picture “almost identical to 338 references, taking significantly less time”, and added it to Kalles Fraktaler in December. It was a new idea in one respect only: Heiland-Allen’s own program already switched pixels to a new reference, but restarting the same reference needs no second one. In tests for this atlas, at widths 3.6·10−31 and 10−35 with the true counts computed directly with 192-bit numbers:

  • without rebasing, about 1% of the pixels were glitched with a good reference; when the reference escaped before the pixels, 36% to 98% could not be computed, and restarting the reference only when it ended still left 28–36% wrong;
  • with rebasing, every reference tried, the center, the nucleus or a corner pixel whose orbit escapes early, gave the same picture;
  • a pixel rebased 6 to 12 times on average, once every 4,000 to 65,000 iterations.

Some pixels still differed from the direct computation, 0.3–2% of them by more than ten iterations, but long-double numbers did no better: these are the oversensitive pixels of the section on digits, not glitches. Rebasing has limits. It relies on the reference starting at the only critical point, 0, so formulas with several critical points need one reference for each, and formulas that alternate between maps need one per phase. And no proof shows that the test |z| < |δ| catches every loss of precision; Heiland-Allen wrote that there is “still room for a numerical analyst” to check why it works so well.

Skipping iterations

Perturbation makes each iteration cheap, but every pixel still does all of them: hundreds of millions at Fractal universe’s location. The remaining speedups use one observation. As long as δ is tiny compared with Zm, the step δ ↦ 2Zmδ + δ2 + Δc is almost linear in δ and Δc, and a chain of linear maps can be multiplied out once, for all pixels, into a single linear map.

Series approximation. Martin’s note already observed that δn, which starts at 0, is at first a power series in Δc alone, δn ≈ AnΔc + BnΔc2 + …, whose coefficients follow from the reference. All pixels can then start at some iteration N instead of 0. Kalles Fraktaler chooses N by comparing the series with eight probe pixels and stopping where they disagree. But the series stops working once the pixels’ orbits spread apart, at the latest after one period of the central minibrot, so it skips at most one period; and agreement at eight points proves nothing about the others. In a test for this atlas at width 10−35 it skipped a third of the iterations, all eight probes agreed, and a quarter of the pixels came out more than ten iterations wrong. Fraktaler 3 replaced it with the next method, and FractalShark does without it.

Bilinear approximation (BLA). If |δ| is much smaller than |2Zm|, the square can be dropped, and the step is the linear map δ ↦ Aδ + BΔc with A = 2Zm and B = 1. The dropped term is at most a fraction ε of the kept one when |δ| < ε|2Zm|, the step’s validity radius; Fraktaler 3 uses ε = 2−24. Two steps combine into one map,

(A2, B2) ∘ (A1, B1) = (A2A1, A2B1 + B2),

valid where the first is and where its output lands inside the radius of the second. Merging neighbors in pairs, then pairs of pairs, gives a table of maps covering 1, 2, 4, 8, … iterations. A pixel takes the longest valid map from where it is, applies it, and repeats; when none is valid, near the moments where Zm passes near 0, it does single perturbation steps and checks for rebasing. Zhuoran hinted at it in November 2021, describing the linear approximation his own program used; Heiland-Allen had it working within two weeks and wrote it up in February 2022 (“Deep zoom theory and practice (again)”); it is in Fraktaler 3, FractalShark and Fractal Zoomer.

Linear approximation (LA). Zhuoran’s own linear approximation, published in his program Imagina, differs from BLA in three ways, as its source code shows. First, it keeps the first square: an entry starting at a reference step a first computes δ(2Za + δ) exactly, and only then applies a linear map. So an entry can start where Z is near 0, exactly where BLA’s radii collapse. Second, entries end where the reference comes close to 0 again, not at powers of two, and higher stages combine these entries into longer ones. Third, a pixel walks from the longest entries down to shorter ones as needed. At Fractal universe’s location the stages came out as 25, 152, 456, 14,896, 44,688, 655,424 and 4,378,360 iterations: apart from the first, the periods of the bar chart above, found without being told any of them. Imagina can even replace a whole period by one step of a small quadratic map in double precision, the renormalization of the minibrots chapter used as a shortcut. FractalShark and Fractal Zoomer have adopted LA.

Most of the remaining work is the last thousands of iterations of each pixel, after it leaves the minibrot’s neighborhood and before it escapes, which must be done one by one.

Limits

Range, again. Not only the pixels’ differences become tiny. The reference values themselves become tiny near their returns to 0, 10−67 at Fractal universe’s location, about the square root of the minibrot’s size, and the coefficients of the skipping tables become huge, so deep tables need floatexp even where the pixels do not. Single-precision mantissas, attractive on graphics processors, fail at some locations.

Iteration counts. Every pixel near a minibrot of period p needs more than p iterations, and periods grow quickly with depth, by factors of 2 to 16 between the rows of the bar chart above. FractalShark’s example views include a period of about 28 billion, rendered at up to 1015 iterations per pixel.

Memory. A stored reference takes 16 to 32 bytes per iteration: a period of 400 million needs 6 to 13 gigabytes. But an orbit near a minibrot repeats its own earlier stretches, so it can be stored as a few “waypoints” and replayed by perturbing it against its own beginning, an idea of Zhuoran’s in Imagina, also used by FractalShark. Ported for this atlas, it stored the 4.4-million-step reference of the deepest test location in 33 waypoints, 920 bytes instead of 70 megabytes.

Time for the reference. Each step of the reference needs the previous one, so it cannot be spread over many processors, except inside each large multiplication. From the measured costs, a reference of 108 iterations takes about 4 minutes at magnification 101000, 2 hours at 1010000 and days beyond 10100000, on one core. At extreme depth this is the real bottleneck: nothing in this chapter makes it faster than p successive multiplications of numbers as long as the depth. FractalShark computes it on a graphics processor and reports a speedup of 70 times over a single-threaded computation at about 500,000 bits.

Correctness. No deep-zoom program has rigorous error bounds. Series approximation, BLA and LA use tolerances found by experiment; Heiland-Allen called the bounds of BLA “a bit handwavy”, and FractalShark’s documentation calls its limits “practical controls on the approximation domain, not a certified bound on the final pixel error”. What can be said is the backward-error statement of the section on digits. In the tests for this atlas, BLA and LA with their default tolerances changed 4–8% of the counts by more than ten iterations, while moving every pixel by 10−8 to 10−6 of its width, with otherwise exact computation, changed 2–11%. A BLA or LA picture is thus as correct as an exact picture of a grid shifted by about a millionth of a pixel. Certified pictures, in which every pixel is proved, are possible only at modest depths, as in the computing chapter.

Other formulas. The Burning Ship and other formulas with absolute values need special care at the lines where the absolute value folds the plane, which act like extra critical points; formulas with several critical points need several references; and LA as Imagina builds it is specific to z2 + c.

Key ideas

  • A zoom to magnification 10k needs about 3.32k bits plus a margin, not more: the computed orbit is the exact orbit of a parameter a tiny fraction of a pixel away (backward error).
  • Arbitrary precision is slow: one step costs 0.24 µs at 10100 and 76 µs at 1010000, against about a nanosecond in hardware.
  • Perturbation computes one reference orbit with many digits and every pixel as an exact small difference δ from it, in hardware numbers with an extended exponent.
  • Glitches arise when a pixel passes much nearer to 0 than the reference; rebasing (Zhuoran, 2021) avoids them by restarting the same reference from 0, so one reference suffices.
  • Bilinear and linear approximation skip long stretches of iterations with precomputed linear maps, hundreds to thousands of times fewer steps; the limits are the reference’s time and memory, and the absence of rigorous error bounds.

Check yourself

  1. About how many bits does a picture 1,000 pixels wide at magnification 10500 need, before the margin?

    log2(10500) ≈ 500 × 3.32 ≈ 1,661 bits to resolve the window, plus log2 1000 ≈ 10 for the pixels across it: about 1,670 bits, or 503 decimal digits. With a margin of a few dozen bits, about 1,700.

  2. Derive the perturbation formula from zn = Zn + δn and c = C + Δc.

    δn+1 = zn+1 − Zn+1 = (Zn + δn)2 + C + Δc − Zn2 − C = 2Znδn + δn2 + Δc. The large terms cancel on paper, not in the computer.

  3. Why can the differences δ be stored as doubles at magnification 1050 but not at 10400, although they need only about 11 bits of mantissa?

    At 10400 the differences are about 10−400, below the smallest double, about 10−308, so they would round to 0. The problem is the exponent range, not the mantissa; a double with a separate exponent (floatexp) or a rescaling solves it.

  4. A pixel has Zm = 0.5 and δ = −0.49. Should it rebase, and what are δ and m afterwards?

    Its value is z = 0.5 − 0.49 = 0.01, and |z| = 0.01 < |δ| = 0.49, so it rebases: δ ← 0.01 and m ← 0. Since Z0 = 0, the pixel’s value Z0 + δ is still 0.01, so nothing has changed except the bookkeeping, and the next steps compare the pixel with the beginning of the reference.

  5. Why does the orbit of a nucleus of period p never escape, and how many values must be stored?

    Its orbit returns to zp = 0 = z0 and then repeats the same p values forever, so it stays bounded. The values Z0, …, Zp−1 describe it completely; a pixel at step n of the reference uses Zn mod p, which is what rebasing at the end of the stored orbit does.

  6. Show that two linear steps δ ↦ A1δ + B1Δc and δ ↦ A2δ + B2Δc combine into one.

    A2(A1δ + B1Δc) + B2Δc = (A2A1)δ + (A2B1 + B2)Δc. The coefficients depend only on the reference, so they are computed once and used by every pixel.
