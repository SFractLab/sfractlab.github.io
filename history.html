From Cayley to the frontier

A short history

Level: IntroductoryBuilds on The Mandelbrot set

The questions behind the Mandelbrot and Julia sets are more than a century old; their pictures are not yet fifty. This chapter follows the subject from a puzzle about Newton’s method, through a prize set in wartime and decades of near silence, to the computer pictures of the late 1970s and the open problems of today.

Cayley’s question

In 1879 Arthur Cayley asked a question about Newton’s method, the classical procedure for finding a root of an equation by repeatedly replacing a guess with a better one. In the complex plane, which starting guesses lead to which root? For quadratic equations the answer is simple: the plane splits along a straight line, the perpendicular bisector of the two roots. “The solution is easy and elegant in the case of a quadric equation,” he wrote, “but the next succeeding case of the cubic equation appears to present considerable difficulty” (Cayley, 1879).

Almost a hundred years later John Hubbard, teaching first- and second-year calculus at Orsay near Paris, was asked by a student how to choose the starting guess. He could not answer, and later recalled that it took him a while to see what the question really meant: what do the regions attracted to each root look like? The answer, which he and his colleagues computed in 1977, was a picture of fractal boundaries (Chazottes and Monticelli’s account). The century between Cayley’s question and Hubbard’s pictures is the story of this chapter.

Local beginnings, 1870–1912

The first results were local: they describe what a map does near a single fixed point. Ernst Schröder studied iteration around 1870. Gabriel Kœnigs proved in 1884 that near an attracting (but not superattracting) or repelling fixed point a change of coordinates turns the map into plain multiplication (Kœnigs, 1884); the orbits chapter uses this to explain how orbits settle. Léopold Leau analyzed fixed points with multiplier 1 in 1897, and Lucjan Böttcher found the normal form near a superattracting point around 1900, the tool behind today’s external rays and equipotentials.

In 1906 Pierre Fatou took the first step toward a global theory. For a rational map such as z ↦ z2/(z2 + 2) he showed that every orbit converges to the attracting point 0 except those starting on a Cantor set, and he observed: « les frontières sont en général de nature compliquée » (“the boundaries are, in general, of a complicated nature”; Comptes rendus 143, p. 546). The tool that would make a general theory possible came from Paul Montel, whose theory of normal families (1907–1912) describes when a family of functions behaves regularly (Montel, 1912).

A prize in wartime, 1915–1920

On 27 December 1915, in the middle of the First World War, the Paris Academy of Sciences set the question for its 1918 Grand Prix des sciences mathématiques: « Perfectionner en un point important l’étude des puissances successives d’une même substitution… » (“to improve in an important point the study of the successive powers of one and the same substitution”). Entries were due at the end of 1917.

Gaston Julia, born in 1893, had been shot in the face in January 1915 as a young second lieutenant at the Chemin des Dames. He lost his nose, wore a leather band across his face for the rest of his life, and did mathematics in hospital (MacTutor biography of Julia). Through 1917 he developed a global theory of iterating rational maps with Montel’s normal families. So, independently, did Fatou, who announced results in short notes in May and December 1917; the December note discusses the example z2 − 1, the map we now call the basilica. To protect his priority, Julia had deposited four sealed envelopes with the Academy between June and December. When they were opened, the Academy found his claim justified. Julia himself spoke only of a « coïncidence curieuse »: both men used precisely Montel’s results (Comptes rendus 165, p. 1098).

Who got what on 2 December 1918. Three memoirs had been entered, by Julia, Samuel Lattès and Salvatore Pincherle; Pincherle’s studied x2 − a for positive real a. The Grand Prix went to Julia, unanimously. Lattès, who had died of typhoid in July 1918, received a “very honorable mention”. Fatou had not entered; on the same day he received a separate prize of 2,000 francs from the Fondation Becquerel for his work on series and on the iteration of rational functions. The report treated the two theories as a simultaneous discovery: « ce n’est pas la première fois, dans l’histoire de la Science, qu’on aura vu deux savants de valeur arriver en même temps, par la même marche, à une même découverte » (“it is not the first time in the history of science that two scholars of merit have arrived at the same discovery at the same time, by the same route”; Comptes rendus 167, p. 814). The record is reconstructed in Michèle Audin’s book on the prize.

Julia’s prize memoir, nearly 200 pages, appeared in the volume of the Journal de mathématiques pures et appliquées dated 1918, which came out in 1919 (Julia, 1918). It contains about thirty figures. Julia knew that his sets were hard to imagine: « Il est assez difficile de se représenter exactement ce que peut être ce continu » (“it is rather difficult to picture exactly what this continuum can be”, p. 169), and he drew a curve built from triangles that was “evidently only a schema, but very suitable for helping the intuition” (p. 170). They drew; what they lacked were accurate pictures.

Fatou’s memoirs of 1919–1920 are the other founding text. They define the set Fatou called F, today’s Julia set, as the set of points near which the iterates do not form a normal family, the definition used today (Fatou, 1919). For polynomials, Fatou proved that the basin of infinity is simply connected exactly when it contains no critical point other than infinity, and that its boundary is a Cantor set when all the critical points are attracted to infinity (Fatou, 1920, p. 85); for z2 + c this is the connected-or-dust dichotomy. He also studied examples with a parameter, such as z2 + a, and showed that the set is a Cantor set when |a| is large; here, Audin remarks, “the Mandelbrot set is within reach.” On p. 73 he asked how the sets vary with the coefficients of the map, and conjectured that maps whose critical points are attracted to cycles are typical, a form of the density-of-hyperbolicity problem that is still open for z2 + c. Neither Fatou nor Julia studied the set of parameters as an object.

Small divisors and quiet decades, 1919–1975

One question survived the 1920s: what happens near a fixed point whose multiplier turns by an irrational angle? In 1919 Julia claimed that such points are never surrounded by a rotation domain. Hubert Cremer showed in 1928 that for many angles no linearizing coordinate exists, the points now called Cremer points; in 1925, in a report on a Berlin seminar about Fatou’s and Julia’s work, he had published schematic drawings of Julia sets. Carl Ludwig Siegel proved in 1942 that for typical angles a rotation domain does exist, refuting Julia’s claim (Siegel, 1942); these are the Siegel disks. Alexander Brjuno found a sharper condition on the angle in the 1960s, and much later Jean-Christophe Yoccoz proved that for quadratic polynomials it is exactly right.

The global theory, however, practically stopped. Fatou died in 1929, at 51, without students; Julia turned to other subjects. A few mathematicians kept working on iteration, among them Pekka Myrberg, who studied the iteration of real quadratic polynomials from the late 1950s, and Hans Brolin, whose 1965 paper on invariant sets was the main postwar account of Julia sets. In 1945 Szolem Mandelbrojt recommended Julia’s memoir to his nephew Benoit Mandelbrot as a source of problems; the young Mandelbrot did not take to it (MacTutor biography of Mandelbrot). Julia died in March 1978, just as the first computer pictures were being made.

Pictures, 1976–1985

What woke the subject was the computer. Adrien Douady put it this way in 1982: « le sujet a pratiquement dormi jusqu’à ces dernières années. C’est la possibilité de faire des expériences numériques sur ordinateur, voire sur un microordinateur, qui l’a réveillé » (“the subject practically slept until recent years; what woke it was the possibility of numerical experiments on a computer, even a microcomputer”; Douady, Séminaire Bourbaki, 1982, p. 39).

The first known picture of the Mandelbrot set is a plot of asterisks on a line printer. Robert Brooks and Peter Matelski were studying groups of Möbius transformations and, in passing, printed a coarse picture captioned “the set of C’s such that f(z) = z² + C has a stable periodic orbit”, that is, with an attracting cycle. At 31 lines of at most 68 characters it shows the cardioid, the larger bulbs and the real axis out to −2, and nothing of the finer structure. Their paper appeared in the proceedings of a 1978 conference at Stony Brook; the authors told the journalist John Horgan that they had not presented it there but had circulated it as a preprint in early 1979. The volume came out in 1981 (Brooks and Matelski, 1981).

Benoit Mandelbrot, then at IBM and visiting Harvard, made the first good pictures in 1979–80, working in the coordinates of the map z ↦ λz(1 − z). His paper of December 1980 was the first published description of the set’s structure: the main body, the bulbs (his “sprouts”) attached to it, and small copies away from it (Mandelbrot, 1980). He drew the wrong conclusion from the islands: “A striking fact, which I think is new, becomes apparent here: FIGURE 1 is made of several disconnected portions” (p. 250). In the same paper he named the filled Julia set of the parabolic parameter c = −3/4 the “San Marco shape”, after the basilica in Venice and its reflection in the flooded square.

Douady and Hubbard set the record straight a year later. Their note in the Comptes rendus, published on 18 January 1982, proved that the set is connected, gave the multiplier coordinates on its hyperbolic components and proved that rational external rays land (Douady and Hubbard, 1982). It credits the pictures: « Benoît Mandelbrot a obtenu à l’ordinateur une très belle image de M, présentant de petits îlots détachés de la composante principale. Ces îlots sont en fait rattachés par des filaments qui échappent à l’ordinateur » (“Benoît Mandelbrot obtained by computer a very beautiful picture of M, showing small islands detached from the main component. These islands are in fact attached by filaments that escape the computer”). The note calls the set simply M; Douady wrote later in 1982 that they called it so after Mandelbrot, and the words “ensemble de Mandelbrot” appear in their Orsay lecture notes of 1984–85, which set out their results in detail (the Orsay notes in English). The 1982 note also asserted, citing a forthcoming paper, that the interior of M consists exactly of the parameters with an attracting cycle. That claim was soon demoted to a conjecture, and it is still open: it is the density of hyperbolicity.

The pictures then reached the public. Heinz-Otto Peitgen and Peter Richter showed their computer graphics in an exhibition in Bremen in January 1984, and pictures of the set in the magazine GEO that June; their exhibition “Frontiers of Chaos” toured the world from 1985 with the Goethe-Institut, and their book The Beauty of Fractals followed in 1986 (Richter’s list of exhibitions). In August 1985 A. K. Dewdney’s column in Scientific American gave readers the algorithm, and its subtitle called the set “the most complex object in mathematics”; many readers began drawing it on their home computers (Dewdney, 1985).

Who discovered the Mandelbrot set?

The question caused a public quarrel. In 1989 Steven Krantz wrote in The Mathematical Intelligencer that the set “was not invented by Mandelbrot” and pointed to Brooks and Matelski (Krantz, 1989); Mandelbrot replied in the same issue that he had published fully, and first. Brooks, Matelski and Hubbard gave Horgan their sides of the story, and all three pointed to Fatou; Hubbard said that his pictures of Newton’s method had been shown to Mandelbrot in late 1978 and that early in 1979 he had explained to him at IBM how to program such plots. John Horgan’s report “Mandelbrot Set-To” collected the voices (Horgan, 1990). William Thurston told Horgan: “Mathematical developments don’t take place single-handedly, and it’s pretty common that things are not named after the first person to develop them.” Brooks told him he had no objection to the name: “It makes more sense than ‘the thing with the big cardioid’.”

A fair summary, which follows Milnor’s account (Milnor’s lectures, Appendix F) for the computer era: Fatou and Julia knew the question and the key fact behind it, but never studied the set of parameters itself. Brooks and Matelski were the first to plot it, crudely and in passing. Mandelbrot made the first good pictures, saw that the set was remarkable and made it famous, though he first thought it fell apart into islands. Douady and Hubbard proved the first theorems and named it after him. Milnor wrote of Mandelbrot: “Although Mandelbrot’s statements in this first paper were not completely right, he deserves a great deal of credit for being the first to point out the extremely complicated geometry associated with the parameter space for quadratic maps.”

New methods, 1982–2000

The pictures raised questions, and new tools answered many of them. Ricardo Mañé, Paulo Sad and Dennis Sullivan showed in 1983 that in any holomorphic family of rational maps the parameters with stable dynamics are open and dense (Mañé, Sad and Sullivan, 1983); for z2 + c the unstable parameters form exactly the boundary of M, as the Mandelbrot chapter explains. Sullivan then proved that rational maps have no wandering domains, settling what he called the Fatou–Julia problem (Sullivan, 1985). He credited the Orsay seminar: “in the fall of 1981 the seminar of Douady and Hubbard at the University of Paris at Orsay on the quadratic family brought to these questions a fresh and stimulating light. The time was ripe to introduce quasiconformal mappings into analytic iteration problems.”

Douady and Hubbard’s theory of polynomial-like maps (1985) explained the small copies of M through renormalization, and William Thurston’s characterization of rational maps with finite critical orbits, whose proof Douady and Hubbard wrote out from his outline, supplied combinatorial models. Douady stated the principle he defended in complex dynamics: « On laboure dans le plan dynamique. On moissonne dans le plan des paramètres » (“we plow in the dynamical plane and harvest in the parameter plane”; Proceedings of the ICM 1994, p. 14).

Around 1990 Yoccoz proved, with a technique now called puzzles, that M is locally connected at every parameter that is not infinitely renormalizable; he never published the proof himself; Hubbard and Milnor wrote it up. He received the Fields Medal in 1994 (IMU, Fields Medals 1994); Douady’s laudation highlighted this work, his proof that Brjuno’s condition is optimal and his work on circle maps. Mitsuhiro Shishikura proved that the boundary of M has Hausdorff dimension 2 (preprint 1991, published 1998; Shishikura, 1998). In 1997 Mikhail Lyubich, and independently Jacek Graczyk and Grzegorz Świątek, proved density of hyperbolicity for real parameters, and Lyubich proved local connectivity at many infinitely renormalizable ones (Lyubich, 1997). Curtis McMullen received the Fields Medal in 1998, for work in complex dynamics and hyperbolic geometry.

The open frontier, 2000–2026

Some old questions have been answered. Xavier Buff and Arnaud Chéritat constructed quadratic Julia sets of positive area (announced in 2006, published in 2012; Buff and Chéritat), completing a program Douady had pursued for years, and Artur Avila and Lyubich found further examples among Feigenbaum maps (2022). Avila’s Fields Medal of 2014 honored his work on dynamical systems and renormalization, and Sullivan received the Abel Prize in 2022.

The question of local connectivity, which Douady and Hubbard first raised in print in 1982, remains open. Lyubich wrote in 2007: “But MLC still resists” (Gazette des Mathématiciens 113, p. 48). Progress continues: Dzmitry Dudko and Lyubich proved local connectivity at the Feigenbaum point and at all infinitely renormalizable parameters of bounded type (Dudko and Lyubich, 2026), Jeremy Kahn, Alex Kapiamba and Lyubich extended it to a further class in a preprint of June 2026, and Dudko received the 2026 Brin Prize in dynamical systems. As of October 2026 MLC and the density of hyperbolicity are both still open; the chapter on open questions lists what is known.

Several of the people in this story have died since 2006: Douady in 2006, Mandelbrot in 2010, Thurston in 2012, and Tan Lei, who proved the similarity between M and Julia sets at Misiurewicz points, and Yoccoz, both in 2016.

Hubbard tree Named by Douady in 1982 after Hubbard, whose idea it was.

Things often said that are not quite right

  • “Fatou and Julia had no pictures.” They drew sketches; Julia’s memoir has about thirty figures. What they lacked were accurate pictures.
  • “Fatou received an honorable mention in 1918.” He did not enter the competition; the mention went to Lattès, and Fatou received a separate prize the same day.
  • “Julia accused Fatou of plagiarism.” Julia wrote of a “curious coincidence”, and the Academy spoke of a simultaneous discovery.
  • “Mandelbrot discovered the Mandelbrot set.” He made the first good pictures and the first description, and made it famous; the first plot, the underlying question and the first theorems are due to others.
  • “Mandelbrot saw from the start that the set is connected.” In 1980 he wrote that it is made of several disconnected portions. According to Milnor, he later decided from better pictures that the islands are joined to the main body by thin filaments; Douady and Hubbard proved that the set is connected.
  • “Yoccoz proved MLC.” He proved it at every parameter that is not infinitely renormalizable; the full conjecture is open.
  • “The Fields Medals of Avila and McMullen were for the Mandelbrot set.” Their citations are broader: renormalization and dynamical systems, complex dynamics and hyperbolic geometry.

Key ideas

  • The global theory of iterating rational maps was created by Fatou and Julia in 1917–1920, using Montel’s normal families; Julia won the Academy’s 1918 Grand Prix.
  • Fatou already knew the critical-point dichotomy for polynomials and asked how Julia sets vary with the parameters, but nobody studied the parameter set itself until computers made it visible.
  • Brooks and Matelski made the first crude plot (for the proceedings of a 1978 conference, published 1981), Mandelbrot the first good pictures (1980), and Douady and Hubbard the first theorems and the name (1982).
  • From the 1980s new methods, from quasiconformal maps to puzzles and renormalization, answered many old questions.
  • Local connectivity of the Mandelbrot set, first asked in 1982, is still open in 2026, though proved at more and more parameters.

Check yourself

  1. What question about Newton’s method did Cayley find easy for quadratic equations and difficult for cubic ones?

    Which starting points in the complex plane lead Newton’s method to which root. For a quadratic equation the plane is divided by a straight line, the perpendicular bisector of the two roots; for a cubic the regions have fractal boundaries, as computer pictures showed a century later.

  2. Who received what from the Paris Academy on 2 December 1918?

    Julia received the Grand Prix des sciences mathématiques. Lattès, who had died that July, received a very honorable mention. Fatou, who had not entered, received a separate prize of 2,000 francs for his work on series and on iteration.

  3. What did Brooks and Matelski’s plot show, and what was missing from it?

    Its caption describes the parameters c for which z2 + c has an attracting (“stable”) cycle. The printout itself, 31 lines of at most 68 characters, shows the main cardioid, the larger bulbs and the real axis out to −2; the small copies, the branching filaments and the fine structure of the boundary were far below its resolution.

  4. Why did Mandelbrot first think that the set was disconnected, and who showed otherwise?

    His pictures showed small islands apparently detached from the main body; the filaments that join them were thinner than his computer could resolve. Douady and Hubbard proved in 1982 that the set is connected.

  5. Which conjecture did the 1982 note of Douady and Hubbard state as if it were a theorem?

    That the interior of M consists exactly of the parameters with an attracting cycle, which is the density of hyperbolicity. It is still open in 2026.
