Structure within structure

Minibrots, tuning & renormalization

Level: IntermediateBuilds on Cardioids, bulbs & periods and Special points

A tiny Mandelbrot-shaped island is more than a visual coincidence. It is the parameter portrait of a slower, local version of the same quadratic dynamics. Its surrounding filaments tell the story of where that copy lives.

What is a minibrot?

A minibrot, also called a baby Mandelbrot set or little Mandelbrot copy, is a subset of the Mandelbrot set that reproduces its organization of components and boundary points through a continuous, reversible correspondence. Mathematicians call that correspondence a homeomorphism. This is a claim about topology: which pieces connect, and how points fit together. It does not require identical angles, lengths, or curvature.

A round bulb by itself is not a whole minibrot. A complete copy includes a distinguished main component, its attached components of many periods, and a corresponding collection of boundary points. A screenshot may expose only a few of these features. The straightening construction gives a precise meaning to the copy even when its finer details are below the image resolution. Lyubich’s treatment of little Mandelbrot copies (§§40–43 of his book) develops this correspondence.

To read a zoom, first locate the largest recognizable component in the small structure. Look for its attachment point and the succession of smaller bulbs. Then separate that internal organization from the extra strands around it. Those strands belong to the larger Mandelbrot set in which the copy is embedded. A minibrot has a local identity and a surrounding context.

A concrete example: the real period-three copy

The conspicuous miniature on the left antenna has a main component of period three. Its center is approximately c = −1.754877666. A center is a parameter at which the critical point 0 lies on the attracting cycle itself. In this case the three-step calculation is

0 → c → c2 + c → (c2 + c)2 + c = 0.

The center is the real root of c3 + 2c2 + c + 1 = 0, as the periodicity chapter derives under centers: an exact target for exploration, independent of colors or visual resemblance.

Near that center, the critical orbit returns near its starting position every three steps. A nearby initial point can travel through three separated neighborhoods before coming back close to where it began. Observe only every third iterate, and the motion has a simpler description. The small copy’s main component represents a fixed-point attractor for this three-step observation, even though the original map has a three-cycle.

Why a quadratic map reappears

Write fc(z) = z2 + c and let gc = fc3. Here the superscript 3 means composition three times. Direct expansion near z = 0 gives

gc(z) = b(c) + A(c)z2 + terms in z4, z6, and z8,
b(c) = (c2 + c)2 + c,   A(c) = 4c(c2 + c).

Both coefficients can be read off the three-step calculation above, the orbit of 0 with z1 = c, z2 = c2 + c and z3 = (c2 + c)2 + c. The constant term b(c) = gc(0) is z3, the orbit of 0 after three steps. The factor A(c) is the derivative along the orbit from z1 to z3. The first step turns a small z into c + z2, a small shift of z1 = c. The next two steps act on that small shift almost linearly, multiplying it by (fc2)′(c) = 2z2 · 2z1 = 2(c2 + c) · 2c = A(c). For small z, the terms in z4, z6 and z8 are much smaller than z2, so the map looks like gc(z) ≈ z3 + Az2.

At the center, the orbit of 0 comes back to 0 after three steps and not before: b = z3 = 0, while z1 and z2 are not 0, and hence A ≠ 0. Set w = Az. In the new coordinate, the image of w is Agc(z):

Agc(z) = Ab + A2z2 + … = w2 + Ab + …

So the leading terms of the return map are those of w ↦ w2 + Ab, with Ab in the place of the parameter. Small changes of the original parameter therefore produce a local quadratic family again. The neglected higher powers, together with the nonlinear way A and b depend on c, are why the copy is distorted rather than an exactly scaled replica.

There is also a distinction between global and local degree. The full three-step polynomial has degree eight. Restricted to an appropriate neighborhood of its critical return, it can act as a two-to-one map onto a larger neighborhood. This restricted behavior, rather than the degree of the full polynomial, is what the next construction captures.

Renormalization and straightening

A quadratic-like map is the local version of z2 + c. It is a map g from a small region U onto a larger region V, both without holes (topological disks), where V contains U together with its edge (the closure of U is compact and lies in V). Like z2, it is two-to-one: every point of V has exactly two preimages in U, counted with multiplicity (it has degree two). Like every polynomial, it is holomorphic, and it carries points near the edge of U to points near the edge of V (it is proper). Points whose orbits leave U are lost; the filled set of g consists of the points whose orbits stay in U forever, the analogue of the filled Julia set. In the period-three example, g = fc3 is quadratic-like on a small region U around 0 for every c of the copy and near it. The expansion above shows the reason: near 0, its leading terms are those of w ↦ w2 + Ab.

Renormalization selects such a restriction of an iterate fp, as g = fc3 above. The Douady–Hubbard straightening theorem says that every quadratic-like map is a quadratic polynomial in disguise: a change of coordinates (a conjugacy) turns it, near its filled set, into some w2 + c′ with the same dynamics. The change of coordinates may bend shapes, but only by a bounded amount (it is quasiconformal), and it bends nothing inside the filled set (Douady and Hubbard call g and w2 + c′ hybrid equivalent). When the filled set is connected, this makes c′ unique.

Now let c run over a small copy, such as the real period-three copy. For each of its parameters, the return map is quadratic-like with a connected filled set, and straightening assigns it a parameter c′ of M. Douady and Hubbard proved that this straightening map c ↦ c′ is one-to-one and continuous in both directions (a homeomorphism, as in the first section). It maps the small copy onto the whole Mandelbrot set; for a satellite copy, it maps the copy without its root onto M without the cusp 1/4 (see below). The copy’s center goes to 0, its main component onto the main cardioid, and its period-two bulb onto the period-two disk. This correspondence is what makes the copy a copy. It keeps how the pieces fit together, but not their exact shapes, which is why the copy is a distorted image of M, as the expansion above already suggested. Douady and Hubbard’s original paper provides the theorem and construction.

Think of two operations: first wait for a return, then use coordinates appropriate to the small neighborhood. Repeating those operations reveals nested dynamics. The original orbit can have complicated excursions between returns while its local return behavior belongs to the familiar quadratic family. This is why similar organizational patterns can recur at very different locations and scales.

A parameter c is renormalizable when this works for some period p ≥ 2: near 0, the return map fcp is quadratic-like, and its filled set is connected, which happens when the orbit of 0 under the return map stays in U. Every parameter of the real period-three copy above is renormalizable, with period 3. In the parameter plane, the parameters that are renormalizable with the same period and the same pattern of returns form one minibrot (for a satellite copy, apart from its root, as explained below), so a parameter is renormalizable when it lies in a minibrot. It is infinitely renormalizable when this happens for infinitely many periods, that is, when it lies in infinitely many minibrots, each inside the one before. The Feigenbaum point is the standard example: it lies in the minibrot whose main component is the period-two disk (a satellite copy, as in the table below), in the one inside it whose main component has period 4, and so on for every power of 2. Renormalization is also the route to the main open question: the local connectivity of M can fail only at infinitely renormalizable parameters (open questions).

Primitive and satellite copies

Type How its main component is situated Example
Primitive Its root is not shared with a hyperbolic component of smaller period. The main component always has a cusp at its root, though it may be too small to see. The real period-three copy near −1.75488.
Satellite Its main component meets a lower-period component at the satellite component’s root. It arises through a rational bifurcation. The copy whose main component is the period-two disk centered at −1.

This distinction also appears in the dynamics: the little filled Julia sets of a primitive renormalization are disjoint, whereas those of a satellite renormalization touch one another at periodic points. For a copy attached directly to the main cardioid they all share one point, the α fixed point; for a copy of period pq attached to a component of period p, they meet q at a time at the p points of a cycle. At a satellite attachment with internal rotation number r/q in lowest terms, a parent component of period p gives rise to a component of period pq. The whole attached limb contains much more than that one component. McMullen’s renormalization discussion (§§7.2–7.4) illustrates the distinction with examples of both kinds.

A technical convention at the boundary: the renormalizable parameters of a satellite copy correspond to the Mandelbrot set without its cusp 1/4, so the copy’s root is missing. Usually the root is added to speak of the closed little Mandelbrot copy. Avila and Lyubich describe this convention in their discussion of renormalization.

Visual shape is a useful clue, not a definition. A satellite copy’s distinguished component may look disk-like even though it corresponds to the main cardioid of the large set. Topological correspondence does not preserve a cusp’s geometry. For the exact main cardioid and the meaning of internal angles, see Cardioids, bulbs & periods.

Tuning: inserting one pattern into another

Tuning describes the embedding of the entire Mandelbrot set into a chosen little copy. If that copy’s main component has period p, a period-q component in the original set corresponds to a period-pq component in the copy. Its local return orbit makes q returns, each requiring p original iterations. Tuning also encodes this substitution in the binary descriptions of external angles. Zakeri’s background section states the period rule and angle substitution.

For the real period-three copy, the recognizable main component has period 3; the analogue of the large period-two bulb has period 6; the period-four descendant has period 12. A small structure shaped like the main cardioid therefore need not represent period one. “Main” is relative to the copy being viewed.

Nested tuning repeats this bookkeeping. A period-three copy inside a period-five copy has a distinguished component of period 15. The product records the number of original steps per return at that level; it says nothing about the size of the copy.

Tuning on external angles

Tuning is also arithmetic on external angles. Let a component have the root angles .⁠(u0) < .⁠(u1), written in binary with repeating blocks u0 and u1 whose length p is the period. In the binary expansion of any angle t, replace each digit 0 by the block u0 and each 1 by u1. If the ray at angle t lands at a point of the Mandelbrot set, the ray at the new angle lands at the corresponding point of the copy (Zakeri, §2; the rule is Douady’s).

For example, the period-two disk has root angles 1/3 = .⁠(01) and 2/3 = .⁠(10). The angle 1/3 = .⁠(01) becomes .⁠(0110), which is 6/15 = 2/5. The root angles of the airplane, 3/7 = .⁠(011) and 4/7 = .⁠(100), turn 1/3 into .⁠(011100) = 28/63 = 4/9. Tuning by the period-two disk again and again climbs the period-doubling cascade: 1/3, 2/5, 7/17, 106/257, 27031/65537, …, the angles of the roots of the components of period 2, 4, 8, 16, 32. They converge to

τ = .0110 1001 1001 0110 … (binary) ≈ 0.412454,

whose digits form the Thue–Morse sequence, since the substitution 0 → 01, 1 → 10 is exactly the rule that generates it. The rays at angles τ and 1 − τ are the only rays whose impressions contain the Feigenbaum point at the end of the cascade (Zakeri, Theorem 3.3 and Lemma 3.4). The impression of a ray is the set of boundary points approached from outside by points whose angle tends to the ray’s angle and whose potential tends to zero.

Decorations, antennas and apparent islands

The connected pieces left when a little copy is removed from the full Mandelbrot set are its decorations; each hangs on the copy at a single point. For the satellite copy whose main component is the period-two disk (figure above), one decoration is the rest of the set, attached at the root −3/4. Another is the antenna from −1.5437, the copy’s version of −2, out to −2 itself, with the period-three copy on it: one copy can lie in a decoration of another. Infinitely many smaller decorations hang at the copy’s other tips. A theorem says that for any fixed positive size, only finitely many of these pieces have diameter at least that size. This controls how decorations accumulate around a copy; it does not say that every visible strand is equally small or regularly spaced. Dzmitry Dudko’s decoration theorem makes the statement precise.

In an image, the surroundings may look like branching trees, spirals, thin antennas, or strings of smaller copies. “Antenna” and “filament” are useful visual terms rather than one universal classification of local geometry. Narrow connections can disappear between pixel centers. A miniature that seems isolated in a raster image is consequently not evidence for a disconnected Mandelbrot set (what the colored image does and does not show).

Close to a minibrot, the decorations often gather into an embedded Julia set: an island that copies a Julia set, with the minibrot at its center and its pattern repeated 2, 4, 8, … times around it.

Self-similarity and universality

Two words come up again and again around minibrots, and each is used for more than one idea: “self-similar” for three, and “universal” for two. This section takes them in turn.

The three meanings of “self-similar” differ in how much they claim:

  • The same topology. A little copy has the same pieces as the whole set, joined in the same way, though its shape is distorted. This is the sense in which the minibrots of this chapter are copies (straightening).
  • Asymptotic similarity. Ever closer magnified views around one point approach a limiting picture, but any two finite views are only approximately alike. Near a Misiurewicz parameter, each magnification by the multiplier of its repelling cycle shows the picture around that point again, more and more exactly the deeper one zooms, and that picture looks like the Julia set there (Tan Lei’s similarity theorem).
  • Exact similarity. The same picture returns after one fixed scale, rotation and translation.

The repetitions in the Mandelbrot set are of the first two kinds: its copies are distorted, and its repetitions around a point become exact only in the limit.

“Universal” names two theorems. Both are about many families of maps sharing a feature, not about one picture repeating itself. The first is Feigenbaum universality. Along the real period-doubling cascade, the distances between the centers of successive components, of periods 1, 2, 4, 8, …, shrink by ratios that approach δ ≈ 4.669201609:

Each center is a root of a polynomial equation, found here with Newton’s method; the first ratio, 3.2185, compares the gap from 0 to −1 with the gap from −1 to −1.3107. Other families of maps with a single quadratic maximum, such as x ↦ r sin(πx), show the same limiting ratio; this is what “universal” means here, and the ratio, not the parameter’s coordinate, is the shared quantity. The scaling comes from renormalization: near the critical point, the second iterate of the map at one stage of the cascade, rescaled, behaves like the map at the stage before. Feigenbaum’s original paper explains the mechanism, and Lyubich’s work on Feigenbaum–Coullet–Tresser universality proves it.

The second is McMullen’s: copies of the Mandelbrot set, distorted only slightly, appear in the parameter space of every family of rational maps that depends holomorphically on its parameters, wherever the dynamics changes (in special families the copies are of a higher-degree Multibrot set instead); Beyond z2 + c states it precisely. Its mechanism is the renormalization and straightening of the earlier section, not the scaling of period doubling.

Key ideas

  • A minibrot is a small topological copy of the Mandelbrot set, the parameter picture of a return map that behaves like a quadratic polynomial.
  • Renormalization looks at every p-th iterate near the critical point; straightening identifies that return map with some z2 + c′; over a minibrot, c ↦ c′ maps it one-to-one and continuously onto all of M.
  • Copies are primitive, with a cusp at the root, or satellite, attached to a lower-period component; most are primitive.
  • Tuning multiplies periods and substitutes binary blocks in external angles; repeating it along the period-doubling cascade leads to the Thue–Morse angle of the Feigenbaum point.
  • “Self-similar” covers topological copies, asymptotic similarity at Misiurewicz points and exact similarity; the Mandelbrot set shows the first two. “Universal” covers the ratio δ of period doubling, shared by many families, and McMullen’s copies of the Mandelbrot set in other families.

Check yourself

  1. In the real period-three copy, what is the period of the component that plays the role of the period-two disk? And of the period-four bulb?

    Periods multiply: 3 × 2 = 6 and 3 × 4 = 12.

  2. Tune the angle 2/3 = .⁠(10) by the period-two disk, whose blocks are 01 and 10. Where does the new ray land?

    Replacing 1 by 10 and 0 by 01 gives .⁠(1001) = 9/15 = 3/5. The rays at 2/5 and 3/5 land together at the root of the period-four bulb, −5/4, the image of −3/4 in the copy grown from the disk.

  3. From the table of centers, estimate the next ratio after 4.6691. What number do the ratios approach?

    They approach δ = 4.6692016…, so the next ratio is about 4.6692. The approach is fast: each step brings the ratio about δ times closer to its limit.

  4. Is a primitive copy attached to a component of smaller period?

    No. Its main component meets no component of smaller period at its root, which is a cusp. Satellite copies are the ones attached to a parent component, like the period-two disk on the main cardioid.
