Angles, addresses and trees

Level: AdvancedBuilds on External rays & angles and Minibrots & self-similarity

Every parameter ray at a rational angle lands, and doubling its angle mirrors the dynamics (external rays and angles). From these two facts grows an exact arithmetic of the Mandelbrot set: which angles land together, how to read the route to a component from its angle, the tree that a map with a finite critical orbit carries, an algorithm that turns an angle into a parameter, and a model of the whole set. The combinatorial parts are computed with fractions, so they are exact.

Which angles land together

At the root of every hyperbolic component of period n ≥ 2 exactly two parameter rays land, both at angles of period n under doubling, and every angle of period n ≥ 2 is one of such a pair (where parameter rays land). The rays chapter lists the pairs up to period 4. Which angle is the partner of a given one? Pierre Lavaurs gave a rule in 1986 that needs nothing but the angles:

  1. Mark the angles of period 2, 3, 4, … on a circle, as fractions of a full turn.
  2. Take the periods in increasing order. Within a period, take the smallest angle that has no partner yet, and join it by a chord to the smallest larger angle of the same period whose chord crosses none drawn before.

Period 2 has only 1/3 and 2/3. In period 3 the nearest angle always works: 1/7 joins 2/7, 3/7 joins 4/7, and 5/7 joins 6/7. The crossing test first matters in period 4. After 1/15 has joined 2/15 and 1/5 has joined 4/15, the angle 2/5 cannot be joined to 7/15 or to 8/15, because both chords would cross the chord from 3/7 to 4/7, so it joins 3/5. Then 7/15 joins 8/15, 11/15 joins 4/5, and 13/15 joins 14/15: exactly the pairs of the rays chapter. That this works in every period is Lavaurs’ theorem. The chords never cross because two wakes are always nested or disjoint (wakes and orbit portraits), and period n contributes one chord for each component of period n: 1, 3, 6, 15, 27, 63, … (counting components).

Kneading sequences

An angle also records a route. Cut the circle at the two angles θ/2 and (θ + 1)/2, the two angles that doubling sends to θ. Call the open half that contains θ the 1-side and the other the 0-side. Then follow θ under doubling and write down, at each step, the side on which the angle lies, or ⋆ when it lands exactly on a cut. The result ν1ν2ν3… is the kneading sequence of θ (Schleicher, Internal addresses in the Mandelbrot set, Definition 2.4).

For θ = 1/7 the cuts are at 1/14 and 4/7, and 1/7 lies between them. Doubling gives 1/7, 2/7 and 4/7: the first two lie on the 1-side, and the third is a cut. The kneading sequence is 11⋆. An angle of period n always meets a cut at step n, because 2n−1θ is one of the two angles that doubling sends to θ. If c lies on the parameter ray at angle θ, the dynamical rays at θ/2 and (θ + 1)/2 both run into the critical point 0 and cut the plane in two; the kneading sequence then says on which side of this cut each point of the critical orbit lies. For a real center or Misiurewicz point it is the classical kneading sequence of the interval map: the sides of 0 visited by c, c2 + c, …, with 1 for the side that contains c, and ⋆ when the orbit returns to 0. For the airplane this gives 10⋆, the kneading sequence of 3/7.

Internal addresses: a road map

Walk from the main cardioid towards the component where the ray at θ lands. At each stage, move to the component of smallest period among those that still separate you from the goal. The periods met in this way form the component’s internal address, introduced by Eike Lau and Dierk Schleicher. It can be read off the kneading sequence (Schleicher, Definition 3.4): start with 1; then each next entry is the first position at which the kneading sequence differs from the periodic repetition of its first S entries, where S is the last entry so far.

For θ = 7/15 the kneading sequence is 100⋆. It differs from 111… at position 2, from 1010… at position 3, and from 1001001… at position 4, so the address is 1 → 2 → 3 → 4. Along the real axis this is the route from the main cardioid through the period-two disk and the period-three window of the airplane to a period-four copy near −1.94.

The address alone does not always decide the component: the 1/3 and 2/3 bulbs both have the address 1 → 3. Recording also which sublimb is taken at each step, as a fraction, gives the angled internal address, 11/3 → 3 for the rabbit’s component and 12/3 → 3 for its mirror image, and the angled address determines the component (Schleicher, Theorem 3.9). Misiurewicz points, such as i, have infinite addresses.

Hubbard trees

When the critical orbit is finite, at a center or a Misiurewicz point, the filled Julia set contains a canonical finite tree. It is the smallest connected set that contains the critical orbit and is made of regulated arcs, which cross the interior of a Fatou component only along internal rays, the curves of constant angle in its Böttcher coordinate. The map sends this Hubbard tree into itself (Douady and Hubbard’s Orsay notes).

The examples show the range. At c = −2 the tree is the whole Julia set, the segment [−2, 2]. For the basilica, c = −1, it is the segment from −1 to 0, through α = (1 − √5)/2. For the airplane it is the real segment from c to c2 + c. For the rabbit it is a tripod: three arms run from α to 0, c and c2 + c, and the map rotates them, as the rotation number 1/3 of the bulb predicts. At the Misiurewicz point i, where the Julia set is a dendrite, the tree is again a tripod, with ends i, −1 + i and −i; the critical point 0 lies on the arm towards −i.

The tree, with the dynamics on it and the angles at its branch points, determines the polynomial, and every abstract Hubbard tree that satisfies an expansion condition is realized by exactly one quadratic polynomial (Alfredo Poirier, Theorem A). Every periodic or preperiodic kneading sequence determines a unique abstract tree (Bruin, Kaffl and Schleicher, 2009). The sequence occurs for a quadratic polynomial exactly when this tree can be drawn in the plane compatibly with its dynamics, that is, when at every periodic branch point the dynamics permutes all arms in a single cycle (Henk Bruin and Dierk Schleicher, 2008).

From an angle to a parameter

Which parameter does an angle name? For a rational angle θ, the answer is a map z2 + c whose critical orbit is finite (it is postcritically finite). Its critical orbit c, fc(c), fc2(c), … can be reached from infinity along curves that come in at the angles θ, 2θ, 4θ, …, and the map carries each of these curves onto the next, as it carries rays. For a strictly preperiodic θ, the curves are the dynamical rays, which land on the critical orbit. For a periodic θ, the rays end on the boundaries of the Fatou components that contain the critical orbit, and the curves continue along internal rays to it.

John Hubbard and Dierk Schleicher’s spider algorithm finds this map by improving a guess for the curves (Hubbard and Schleicher). A spider has one leg for each angle of the orbit θ, 2θ, 4θ, …: a curve from infinity, coming in at that angle, to a foot. The first spider has straight legs that end on the unit circle. Since the map must carry each leg onto the next, each step replaces every leg by the preimage, under z ↦ z2 + c, of the leg of the next angle, with c taken to be the foot of the first leg; the branch of the square root is fixed at infinity by the angle. The feet converge to the critical orbit. When θ is periodic, the first foot converges to the center of the component at whose root the ray lands; when θ is strictly preperiodic, it converges to the Misiurewicz point where the ray lands. That this converges, and to a unique answer, rests on Thurston’s characterization of rational maps (Douady and Hubbard’s proof, 1993).

A short implementation, with legs drawn as polygons, gives in a fraction of a second −1 for θ = 1/3, the rabbit’s center −0.1225611669 + 0.7448617666i for 1/7, the airplane’s −1.7548776662 for 3/7, the point i for 1/6, and −0.1010963638 + 0.9562865108i for 9/56, where three rays land (the Misiurewicz table).

A model of the whole set

Draw all of Lavaurs’ chords, add the limits of sequences of chords, and the result is William Thurston’s quadratic minor lamination, from his work on the dynamics of iterated rational maps, circulated as a preprint in the 1980s and published in 2009 (Thurston). Collapsing each chord to a point, together with each region bounded by finitely many chords, turns the closed disk into a compact, connected and locally connected space: Adrien Douady’s pinched disk, a combinatorial model of M. The regions bounded by infinitely many chords survive as open sets and model the hyperbolic components; the one bounded by the chords of all the primary bulbs is the main cardioid.

What is proved about the model, and what is not, must be kept apart. The key notion is the fiber of a point of M (Schleicher’s term): the set of points of M that cannot be separated from it by cutting along two rational parameter rays that land at the same point, or along two rays that land on the boundary of the same hyperbolic component, joined through that component. For example, two points in different limbs of the main cardioid are separated by the two rays that land at the root of one of the limbs, so they lie in different fibers. The model sees M only through such cuts, since its chords join the angles of rays that land together, so it cannot tell apart the points of one fiber. There is always a continuous map from M onto the model that collapses each fiber to a point, and all rational combinatorics, which rays land together and which wake lies inside which, is exactly the same in M and in the model (Zakeri, §2; Schleicher, fibers and local connectivity). M is homeomorphic to the model exactly when every fiber is a single point, which is the case exactly when M is locally connected: the open MLC conjecture. Density of hyperbolicity is equivalent to the weaker statement that no fiber has interior. Fibers are known to be points at every Misiurewicz point and at every point on the boundary of a hyperbolic component.

Core entropy

How chaotic is the map on its Hubbard tree? Its topological entropy there, the core entropy, measures how fast the number of distinguishable orbits grows. For a finite tree it is the logarithm of the largest eigenvalue of a matrix that records which edges each edge covers. For the airplane the tree has two edges, from c to 0 and from 0 to c2 + c; the first covers both, and the second covers the first. The matrix (1 1; 1 0) has largest eigenvalue the golden ratio φ, so the core entropy is log φ ≈ 0.4812.

For real parameters the core entropy is the entropy of the real map, which falls monotonically from log 2 at −2 to 0 at the Feigenbaum point and stays 0 beyond it, as c increases; this was first proved in the 1980s with complex methods, and Masato Tsujii gave a short proof in 2000 (Tsujii). Thurston proposed studying core entropy over the whole set, as a function of the external angle, and gave an algorithm to compute it. Giulio Tiozzo proved that it depends continuously on the angle (Tiozzo, 2016), as did Dzmitry Dudko and Dierk Schleicher by other means (Dudko and Schleicher, 2020), and Yan Gao proved Thurston’s algorithm correct (Gao, 2020).

Key ideas

  • Lavaurs’ rule, joining angles of each period in turn without crossings, finds every pair of periodic parameter rays that land together.
  • The kneading sequence of an angle records, step by step under doubling, on which side of the two cuts at θ/2 and (θ + 1)/2 the angle lies.
  • The internal address, read from the kneading sequence, lists the periods met on the way from the main cardioid; with angles added, it determines the component.
  • A map with a finite critical orbit carries a Hubbard tree in its filled Julia set; the tree, with its dynamics and the angles at its branch points, determines the parameter.
  • From a rational angle, the spider algorithm computes the center of the component at whose root the ray lands, or the Misiurewicz point where it lands.
  • Collapsing Thurston’s lamination gives a model of M with the same rational combinatorics; it is homeomorphic to M exactly if MLC holds.

Check yourself

  1. In period 4, why can Lavaurs’ rule not join 2/5 to 7/15?

    The chord from 2/5 to 7/15 would cross the chord from 3/7 to 4/7, drawn in period 3: 3/7 lies between 2/5 and 7/15, but 4/7 does not. The next candidate, 8/15, fails for the same reason, so 2/5 joins 3/5, and its chord passes around the chord from 3/7 to 4/7.

  2. Compute the kneading sequence of 3/7.

    The cuts are at 3/14 and 5/7, and 3/7 lies between them, so that open half is the 1-side. Doubling gives 3/7 (1-side), 6/7 (beyond 5/7, the 0-side) and 12/7 − 1 = 5/7, a cut. The kneading sequence is 10⋆.

  3. Read the internal address from the kneading sequence 101⋆ of the angle 2/5.

    Start with 1. The sequence differs from 111… first at position 2, so the next entry is 2. It differs from the repetition 1010… of its first two entries at position 4, where it has ⋆, so the address is 1 → 2 → 4: from the main cardioid through the period-two disk to the period-four bulb on it.

  4. Why does the basilica’s Hubbard tree pass through α?

    The tree must connect 0 and −1, the two points of the critical orbit, and their Fatou components touch only at α = (1 − √5)/2 ≈ −0.618: the regulated arc runs along internal rays from 0 to α and from α to −1, which together make the segment [−1, 0].

  5. Why is the core entropy of Douady’s rabbit 0?

    Each of the three arms of the tripod is mapped one-to-one onto the next arm, since only the arm ending at 0 contains the critical point, and that only at its end. The matrix that records which edge covers which is a permutation matrix, whose largest eigenvalue is 1, and log 1 = 0.

  6. Why does the core entropy of the airplane come out as the logarithm of the golden ratio?

    The edge from c to 0 is mapped onto the whole tree from c to c2 + c, covering both edges, and the edge from 0 to c2 + c is mapped onto the edge from c to 0. The matrix (1 1; 1 0) has characteristic polynomial λ2 − λ − 1, whose largest root is the golden ratio (1 + √5)/2.
